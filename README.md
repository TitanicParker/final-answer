The patient entered the 2017 admission with a unified disabling problem involving his gait, feet, toe-clawing and bodily tension. Parkinsonism was identified, but the relationship between the feet and the neurological syndrome remained expressly “Not clear,” while the observed lower-limb posture appeared dystonic. The admission therefore did not begin with two established conditions. It began with one presentation whose internal relationship required resolution.

A senior-supported treatment architecture was nevertheless established. Ordinary dopaminergic treatment was to be withheld for as long as possible, while the feet passed operationally toward Podiatry. Yet the same documentary package recorded the dystonic-looking posture, raised PRKN/PARK2 and enclosed Professor Counihan’s own article connecting juvenile PRKN disease with dystonia, symmetry and excellent levodopa responsiveness. The record contained the observation, the relevant hypothesis and the specialist knowledge capable of connecting them, but did not visibly allow that connection to govern the patient-specific treatment of the feet.

Before discharge, the patient says that he challenged this unresolved partition. His Protest did not reject the Parkinsonism diagnosis. It asserted that the dominant disabling problem remained unexplained and that referring the feet onward did not constitute clinical resolution. If established, the Protest was therefore material to the adequacy of discharge, the meaning of the foot disposition, the completeness of the treatment plan and whether the patient accepted the course attributed to him.

The authoritative discharge synthesis did not preserve that challenge. Instead, it recorded a clear senior-backed strategy, Podiatry, “good understanding” and a plan incorporated “as per above.” The document remained open long enough to absorb the post-discharge DaTSCAN as expected confirmation, but not the pre-discharge Protest as evidence that the plan had been contested. If the Protest was knowingly omitted to avoid reopening an already commissioned discharge course, that omission converted a contested decision into an apparently accepted foundation.

Later clinicians then encountered the completed foundation rather than the unresolved bedside state that preceded it. They could act reasonably and in good faith while still working inside an altered decision environment. The feet could be treated as pain, pressure, neuropathy, Podiatry or a separate peripheral problem; medication could be adjusted prospectively; and recurring symptoms could be managed locally without anyone being assigned responsibility for asking whether the recurrence impeached the original partition. No downstream conspiracy was required. Documentary dependency was sufficient.

Neurological evidence nevertheless returned repeatedly through the feet. Procyclidine improvement and withdrawal deterioration affected the feet, tension and gait. Later records described the foot movement as Parkinsonian, documented toe-curling, possible dystonia and OFF-related pain, and connected walking failure with inadequate levodopa effect. By 2025, Parkinson medication was acknowledged to relieve the feet, additional Sinemet was expected to provide further relief, Sinemet CR was prescribed specifically for the severe morning foot problem, and the resulting relief was described as profound. These events generated treatment changes, but the supplied record does not show them travelling backwards to reconstruct the 2017 foundation.

The patient therefore experienced more than delayed symptom relief. He was required to live and seek care within a history that did not preserve the moment when he had challenged the unresolved problem. The institution possessed the authoritative account; the patient was left to carry the omitted disagreement in his memory and the unresolved question in his body. Each recurrence required him to explain again what the foundational record made appear settled, while the passage of time increased the authority of the written account and weakened his ability to recover the omitted state.

The consequences ultimately reached documented language of death. In 2023, an urgent GP referral described the bilateral foot and lower-leg suffering as extreme and “causing death wish,” expressed concern that the patient was not doing well and requested review “before we get into trouble here.” In 2025, the neurological record described “murder in the feet,” the first 150 steps as “torture,” and recorded the statement “I won’t live with it.” This supports a two-year span of suicide-relevant documentation associated with the unresolved foot suffering, although it does not by itself prove uninterrupted suicidal intent throughout that period.

When the patient complained, the response acknowledged that his pain and discomfort had been documented but stated that the clinician had not understood that this was how he felt. It did not visibly reconstruct the earlier death-wish warning as part of the causal and governance history. Instead, it relied upon the longitudinal record to show that Sinemet responsiveness had long been understood, that treatment restraint had been deliberate, that the patient had received Sinemet since 2021 and that the eventual profound relief fitted the established approach.

That created the decisive asymmetry. The record’s continuity was treated as sufficiently reliable to demonstrate institutional knowledge, foresight and a coherent treatment strategy, but not given equivalent effect as evidence that the patient’s suffering had escalated to recorded death-wish language or that the original unresolved partition required reconstruction. Continuity supported the institution prospectively and defensively; contradiction and patient danger were not permitted the same retrospective force.

The complaint process therefore risked becoming the final tooth of the ratchet. Later care conducted within the inherited framework was used to validate that framework, even though its apparent continuity may have been a product of documentary descent rather than independent reconsideration. The future generated by the foundation was treated as evidence that the foundation had been sound, instead of being examined as evidence of the foundation’s reach and consequences.

The case is accordingly not that every later clinician was negligent, dishonest or motivated by the original omission. It is that a material patient challenge may have been knowingly removed at the precise moment when recording it would have required reconsideration of a senior-backed discharge plan. That act created an apparently uncontested foundation; later care became materially dependent upon it; neurological evidence repeatedly returned without visibly reopening it; the patient’s suffering escalated to suicide-relevant documentation; and the complaint response ultimately relied upon the record’s resulting continuity to support the course that had generated that continuity.

The central case proposition is therefore this: motive belongs at the omission; dependency explains the subsequent record; injury belongs to the patient; and circularity appears when the institutional future produced by the omitted Protest is relied upon as proof that the originating course was coherent. If knowing omission is established, later dependent conduct cannot authenticate the foundation merely through repetition. It must be severed from that foundation and independently revalidated from the last reliable point before the Protest disappeared.

# Watching the Story Continue

## A method for reading the post-March 2025 record

The later record should be read as two chronologies running side by side:

1. **the institutional chronology** — what clinicians, QPS, incident review, data-protection staff and other bodies recorded or decided; and
2. **the patient knowledge chronology** — what the patient knew, experienced, suspected, obtained and only later became able to reconstruct.

The central rule is simple:

> **Never give an earlier actor knowledge they did not yet possess.**

The patient of 2017 is not the investigator of 2026. The complainant of 19 March 2025 is not the same informational actor as the patient who had obtained FOI material by late May. Later genetics cannot be inserted backward into earlier decision-making.

## 1. Begin with bodily knowledge

Before March 2025 the patient possessed years of direct bodily information: pain, gait difficulty, foot behaviour, medication effects and the experience of improvement or deterioration.

That information was real even when its significance was not yet understood.

The feet are particularly important because they repeatedly served as a practical readout of the condition. This does not establish one mechanism. It establishes that the patient's own information about the disease was often generated through the feet.

A useful rule is:

> **Experience can precede interpretation.**

The patient can know that a tablet changes his feet before he knows how that fact relates to the historical record.

## 2. March 2025 is a rupture, not instant omniscience

The Sinemet CR response produces a new fact for the patient: profound relief in the same bodily territory that had remained troublesome for years.

That event destabilises the inherited explanation, but it does not instantly reveal the documentary history.

The formal complaint of 19 March is therefore a high-value epistemic document. It records what the patient knew and suspected at that moment. It also expressly says that he did not have every internal note, referral or item of correspondence.

Do not correct that document using later knowledge. Its incompleteness is part of its evidential value.

## 3. Distinguish notice from proof

The March complaint put the institution on notice of:

- profound treatment response;
- suspected anchoring and failure to reassess;
- possible hierarchical influence;
- prolonged pain and harm;
- and a request for review, disclosure, explanation and preservation.

It did not prove those hypotheses.

This distinction matters because later records should be examined partly for how the institution responded to what it had been asked to investigate, not merely for whether the patient's initial theory was already correct in every detail.

## 4. Track what each institutional response adds

For every later document, ask:

- What new fact does this document establish?
- What prior proposition does it merely repeat?
- What source does the author rely upon?
- What question is answered?
- What question remains untouched?
- Does the document create a new institutional consequence?

For example, a clinician's response can establish the treatment rationale the clinician says governed care. It does not independently establish that the original foot/gait relationship was fully resolved.

A QPS closure establishes how the complaint was classified and handled. It does not by itself establish that the underlying clinical account was complete.

An incident-review conclusion establishes that the review reached that conclusion. Its independence depends on the evidence actually examined.

## 5. Mark the FOI transition

By late May 2025 the patient states that he has obtained internal documents through FOI.

That is a major change in the knowledge state.

From this point the patient can begin to compare:

- what he remembers;
- what he experienced;
- what clinicians wrote at the time;
- what clinicians later said they had intended;
- what the portable discharge record preserved;
- and what material appears absent or unavailable.

The case shifts from bodily contradiction to documentary reconstruction.

## 6. Treat the record as a dependency graph

Later records should not be counted as independent votes.

Build the chain.

For each proposition identify whether it arises from:

- direct observation;
- patient report;
- prior clinical note;
- diagnostic coding;
- a summary of earlier material;
- later review of primary evidence;
- or later repetition of an already inherited conclusion.

Then ask whether the later actor actually had the means to re-test the disputed point.

This method avoids two opposite errors:

- assuming continuity proves correctness; and
- assuming continuity proves dependence.

Either proposition has to be demonstrated.

## 7. Keep the Protest in its proper evidential category

The reported pre-discharge Protest is later recollection. It should not be rewritten as a 2017 recorded fact.

But it should also not be discarded merely because the surviving clinical record is silent.

The correct method is to track:

- when the recollection first appears in the surviving corpus;
- how detailed and stable it is;
- what contemporaneous facts make it plausible or implausible;
- what potential witnesses or records might test it;
- what categories of potentially relevant institutional material survive or do not survive;
- and whether the final account would be materially different if the event were established.

## 8. Preserve the two meanings of accuracy

Later data-rights disputes reveal a crucial analytical distinction.

A historical record can be accurate in the sense that it faithfully records what a clinician thought at the time.

That does not automatically mean it is complete as a representation of the event.

Always distinguish:

> **historical-opinion accuracy**

from

> **event completeness and present contextual accuracy.**

This distinction is especially important where a disputed diagnosis, omitted disagreement or later correction continues to influence current decision-making.

## 9. Track restoration of symmetry as a sequence

The restoration of informational symmetry is not one disclosure event.

It occurs in stages:

**March 2025:** bodily contradiction.  
**19 March:** complaint and explicit acknowledgement of incomplete access.  
**April-May:** institutional responses and FOI acquisition.  
**June-September:** access, rectification, restriction, incident-review and missing-record inquiries reveal institutional positions and data topology.  
**Late 2025-January 2026:** the patient increasingly reconstructs the relationship between the clinical history, governance history and record architecture.  
**February 2026:** genetics confirms PRKN-related Parkinsonism and dystonia and constrains the retrospective narrative.

At each date, write only what the actor could then know.

## 10. Use later clinical evidence to constrain, not rewrite, the past

The genetic result is powerful because it prevents a false binary. It confirms that Parkinsonism and dystonia can belong to the same underlying Parkin disorder.

It does not prove that every foot symptom was dystonia, or that clinicians in 2017 should have known the eventual result.

Its proper retrospective use is to show that a record containing both Parkinsonian and dystonic information cannot now be read as though those categories were necessarily mutually exclusive.

## 11. The final analytical question

After the chronology has been reconstructed, ask:

> **When the patient finally possessed enough of the record to see how the institution had represented the originating episode, what became visible that he could not have known while living inside the management plan created in 2017?**

That question ties the clinical record to the information record.

It is also the safeguard against hindsight. The answer must emerge from the dated sequence, not be imposed on it.

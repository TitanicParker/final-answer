# Watching the Story Continue

## The Institution, the Patient, and the Record After March 2025

This document is not a description of what an analytical model *could* do with the case. It does the analysis.

The repository already contains the originating clinical story: a 2017 admission in which Parkinsonism was investigated extensively; the feet, toe-clawing, pain and dystonic-looking gait remained incompletely integrated; a Podiatry pathway survived; ordinary dopaminergic treatment was deliberately restrained; and the completed discharge account presented a coherent plan, senior provenance and patient understanding. The patient says that before discharge he protested that the principal problem had not actually been dealt with. That Protest is absent from the surviving authoritative account.

If the Protest occurred, the omission matters because it changed the story available to the future.

The later documents let us watch what happened when that story finally encountered the patient again.

From March 2025 onward, there are effectively two narratives moving through the same file.

One is the **institutional narrative of continuity**: the diagnosis and treatment course were coherent in their own time; later treatment changes fit that course; complaint handling can rely upon contemporaneous records; historical professional opinion remains an accurate record of what was thought; later diagnostic change need not reopen earlier governance conclusions.

The other is the **patient's narrative of discovery**: the tablet worked; therefore something important about the previous years had not been understood; the hospital should investigate; the record does not contain what he remembers saying in 2017; the missing event may be load-bearing; there were witnesses; other controllers may hold traces; the disputed old story is still producing present consequences; therefore accuracy, access, restriction and preservation become ways of forcing the past back into the present.

The value of the post-March documents is that these two stories can be watched developing in real time.

---

## 1. Before March 2025, the institution already possessed a finished story

By the time the patient reached March 2025, the institutional history had been accumulating for nearly eight years.

The 2017 record had identified Parkinsonism and had created a senior-backed treatment architecture. The feet were documented, the gait was described as suggestive of dystonic posture, structural abnormalities were recorded, peripheral explanations were entertained, and the relationship was left "Not clear." Podiatry became the practical foot pathway.

The patient says that this was not how the admission felt from inside the bed.

His evidence is that before discharge he told the team, through the NCT and "bald tyres" analogy, that they had investigated him extensively while leaving the thing that actually met the ground unresolved. Four trainees were allegedly present; one allegedly fetched the neurologist.

That event is not preserved in the completed discharge synthesis.

The institutional record therefore entered the future without the patient's contemporaneous objection that the central problem had not been resolved.

That distinction is essential to what happens later. The institution carried forward a completed narrative. The patient carried forward an unrecorded disagreement.

For years those two things could coexist because the patient did not yet know that the absence of his disagreement might matter structurally.

---

## 2. 10–14 March 2025: the patient's body breaks the continuity before his argument does

The decisive rupture begins on 10 March 2025.

The record describes the feet as the dominant problem. The patient describes "murder in the feet," the first 150 footsteps as "torture," and says:

> "I won't live with it."

That statement comes after earlier warning. In 2023, the GP had already described the bilateral foot and lower-leg suffering as "causing death wish" and had asked for review "before we get into trouble here." The March consultation therefore did not occur against a background of ordinary chronic discomfort. It occurred after a documented escalation in physical and psychological danger.

Sinemet CR was prescribed on 10 March. The patient collected it on 13 March. On 14 March he woke with what the later clinician response itself called "profound relief."

This is the first event in the 2025 story that neither side can reduce to wording.

Something happened in the patient's body.

The patient did not yet know the eventual documentary theory. He did not know that the Protest would become central. He did not know which records existed, what the discharge synthesis contained, what the institution would later rely upon, or which controller might hold teaching data.

He knew the tablet had changed the feet.

That changed his view of the past before it changed the record.

---

## 3. 19 March: the patient's first response is notification, not accusation

Five days after the reported profound relief, the patient submitted the formal complaint of 19 March.

This document is especially valuable because it freezes his understanding before later discovery.

He alleges diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess and possible hierarchical influence. He asks the obvious retrospective question:

> "Why was this possibility not explored earlier, given the magnitude of harm endured?"

But he also expressly says:

> "I make no presumptions about individual intent or malice."

He asks for independent review, disclosure, explanation and preservation.

This matters enormously to the later evidential story.

The patient does **not** begin with a claim that he has discovered a knowing omission in 2017. He does not identify the four trainees. He does not present a cross-controller theory. He does not allege that the discharge account commissioned eight years of documentary dependency.

He asks the institution to investigate because he does not yet possess the material required to know what happened.

The March complaint therefore records a patient at the beginning of discovery.

That makes the later evolution of his account observable rather than merely asserted retrospectively.

---

## 4. The first institutional answer is continuity

The treating clinician's response does not treat the March medication event as requiring reconstruction of the original 2017 foundation.

Instead, the response places it inside the existing longitudinal story.

The institution's position is, in substance, that Sinemet responsiveness was not a surprising contradiction because dopamine responsiveness had long been understood; treatment restraint was deliberate; pain and discomfort had been documented; Sinemet had been used in the later course; and the profound response could therefore be understood within the established management history.

The same response says that the clinician had not understood that this was how the patient felt.

That pairing is central.

The institution claims continuity of clinical knowledge while disclaiming full knowledge of the lived severity.

The patient's emerging position is almost the reverse: he believes the severity and the neurological relevance of the feet were repeatedly communicated, but the institutional record did not permit those facts to travel backward far enough to alter the original story.

This is the first post-March appearance of the pattern later stated more formally by the repository:

**new information is capable of being absorbed prospectively without being allowed to reconstruct the foundation retrospectively.**

---

## 5. 15 April: the institutional story becomes a governance story

On 15 April, QPS classifies the complaint as relating solely to clinical judgment, forwards it to the treating consultant, and treats the clinician response as sufficient to close the complaint pathway then being used.

That is a second transformation of the same history.

The original clinical story has now produced a complaint-handling story.

The patient had asked for an independent examination of how the course arose. The institution responds through a mechanism that places substantial explanatory weight on the existing clinical account and the clinician responsible for it.

That does not establish bad faith.

It does something more important for this repository: it shows the **behaviour of the inherited story when challenged**.

Rather than being dismantled and reconstructed, it is converted into the evidential basis of an institutional answer.

The record begins to validate itself through a new document.

---

## 6. 18 April onward: the patient starts building a record of the challenge itself

The patient's behaviour changes after closure.

On 18 April he asks that his rebuttal be retained in the file. He wants later readers to encounter the unresolved questions beside the institutional closure rather than encountering closure alone.

This is an important change in behaviour.

In March, he trusted the hospital to investigate.

By mid-April, he has begun to understand that **how the dispute is recorded may determine how the dispute is later understood**.

The correspondence from this point starts performing a second function. It is still asking for help, explanation and safer care, but it is also creating timestamps, preserving objections, identifying unanswered questions and preventing silence from becoming the only official account.

The patient is beginning to construct a counter-record.

That behaviour should not be read backwards into March as though it was his plan all along.

It develops because of what happens after he complains.

---

## 7. April and May: the patient is not merely litigating the past; he is still trying to obtain safe treatment

The documentary story becomes distorted if the patient is portrayed as having moved immediately from complaint into legal strategy.

He had not recovered.

The records show continuing psychological distress, efforts to obtain support, medication problems, attempts to understand whether dystonia could explain the feet, and concern about remaining under the same neurological pathway.

He asks for a different treatment arrangement. He raises the possibility of movement-disorders expertise. He challenges a future appointment under the same treating clinician because he no longer feels safe in that relationship.

He is simultaneously doing three things:

1. trying to understand what happened;
2. trying to preserve what the institution is doing with his complaint;
3. trying to obtain treatment from somewhere he trusts.

That combination matters because it helps explain why the later GDPR campaign is not merely procedural escalation.

The record itself has become part of the patient's care problem.

If downstream clinicians continue to work from the same inherited history, changing clinician without changing the information environment may not solve the problem.

---

## 8. 26–30 May: the patient starts challenging not only the clinical history but the history of the complaint

By late May, the patient is documenting what he says happened during the complaints process itself.

He states that on 28 March he asked for Stage 2 escalation. He challenges the later classification, routing and closure. On 30 May the matter moves into the Incident Management Framework, with a Preliminary Assessment Report to be considered by SIMT.

This is a real institutional escalation and should be acknowledged as such.

But it creates a new analytical question:

**what information state enters the higher-level review?**

If the review begins from the inherited clinical record plus the clinician's defence plus the earlier complaint classification, then increased seniority does not necessarily equal increased independence from the original narrative.

The repository should therefore watch not merely whether a process became more serious, but whether the **story being processed changed**.

---

## 9. June: the patient discovers that accuracy may be a route back into the past

The shift into GDPR begins on 12 June with Article 16.

The patient asks for rectification and annotation of the clinical record.

This is not yet primarily an attempt to find the four students.

It is an attempt to solve the problem that has emerged since March: the historical record does not visibly contain a meaningful **before and after**.

For the patient, March 2025 divided the story.

Before: the inherited explanation governed.

After: a targeted dopaminergic intervention produced profound relief and forced him to reconsider the preceding years.

Article 16 becomes a way to say: if the old diagnosis or description is retained as history, the record must also be capable of showing the later contradiction, the omitted presenting problem, and the fact that the older account is now disputed or incomplete.

Three days later, on 15 June, he applies the same logic to the QPS record.

That move is highly revealing.

He has realised that the institution is creating **new personal history about how it handled the old personal history**.

He therefore tries to rectify not only the clinical account but the governance account.

The dispute has moved from "what happened medically?" to "what story is each institutional layer now creating about what happened?"

---

## 10. 13–17 June: institutional containment and patient rerouting

During this period QPS asks the patient to refrain from further correspondence while the case is being reviewed and collated.

There may be ordinary administrative reasons for such a request.

Its documentary effect is nevertheless important.

The institution continues processing internally while the patient—the principal source of contradiction—is asked to stop adding material to that channel.

The patient's response is not to disappear.

He reroutes.

He sends additional material to the DPO and Ombudsman and changes how QPS is copied.

That behavioural change is part of the case.

The patient is learning the institutional map in real time.

When one route appears unable or unwilling to carry the information where he wants it to go, he searches for another route with a different statutory function.

---

## 11. 7 July: the patient finally turns from the record to the room

The 7 July Article 15 request is one of the most important documents in the post-March chronology because it shows a new level of understanding.

The patient now seeks records of the July 2017 bedside teaching interaction: student logs, teaching material, ward-round records, supervisory material and trainee assignments.

Why?

Because by this point the Protest is no longer merely something he remembers saying.

He is beginning to understand that its absence may be **load-bearing**.

If the Protest was the event in which he told the institution before discharge that the feet had not been properly investigated, then its omission helps explain why the later discharge account can look uncontested.

And then comes the practical realization:

**there were allegedly four other people there.**

The patient's evidential strategy changes immediately once that fact becomes important.

He no longer asks only:

> Why isn't this in my chart?

He begins asking:

> Who were the people in the room, what institutional traces did their presence create, and can the event be reconstructed outside the document that omitted it?

This is the moment GDPR becomes a bridge from documentary history back toward physical reality.

---

## 12. July: the patient becomes increasingly precise about institutional categories

The July correspondence shows the patient learning GDPR while using it.

He distinguishes Article 15 access from Article 16 rectification. He presses the DPO on deadlines and classification. He consolidates the Article 16 request. He contacts the DPC when he believes the controller has not complied.

This is worth observing as behaviour, not merely law.

The patient is trying to prevent each request from being absorbed into a general category such as "your GDPR correspondence."

Why does that matter to him?

Because by now **classification itself has become part of the problem**.

The original complaint was classified as clinical judgment.

His challenge to that classification became another procedural dispute.

Now he is insisting that access, rectification and later restriction remain distinct because each right asks a different question of the institution.

The patient is learning that if the institution controls the category, it may also control what question gets answered.

---

## 13. 4 August: the patient tries to stop the old story creating a new one

Article 18 is the next conceptual step.

By 4 August, SIMT is in motion while the accuracy dispute is unresolved.

The patient invokes restriction of processing and asks for a data-clean protocol, data sources, versioning and audit trails.

This is not simply "another GDPR complaint."

The patient has now identified a recursive danger.

If the disputed 2017-derived history is used to conduct a serious incident review, that review can generate a new authoritative institutional artifact.

The old record can therefore reproduce itself through governance.

Article 18 is the patient's attempt to interrupt that process:

**before you use the disputed history to decide whether the history was safe, determine what information you are using and whether it is accurate enough to govern the review.**

This is the point at which the patient's theory of the record becomes recognisably systemic.

It did not exist in this form on 19 March.

The documents show him arriving at it.

---

## 14. 5 August: the search for 2017 encounters the limits of institutional memory

The hospital's response to the July Article 15 request says that several categories of teaching, student or ward-round material are not held and directs the Podiatry element toward Community Healthcare West.

That response does not prove that the Protest occurred.

It does not prove destruction.

But it creates a striking asymmetry.

The authoritative clinical synthesis survives.

Some of the peripheral traces that might independently test its completeness are said not to be held.

The patient therefore discovers a practical feature of documentary power:

**the record that won in 2017 has survived better than the evidential environment capable of challenging it.**

That is precisely why the later search expands beyond one controller.

---

## 15. 3 September: the institutional story acquires certification

The SIMT outcome concludes that there was "no deficit in the care provided."

This is a major event because the inherited clinical history has now produced a governance conclusion.

The issue is not simply that the patient disagrees with SIMT.

The analytical question is what work the longitudinal record performs inside that conclusion.

The later care history is used as evidence that treatment decisions were appropriate and coherent. Sinemet use appears in the institutional explanation. The existing course is treated as a meaningful demonstration of what clinicians knew and why they acted.

But if later care was itself materially dependent on the 2017 information state, then later continuity cannot automatically serve as independent proof of the foundation.

That is the recursive point.

The future produced by the story begins to certify the story.

---

## 16. 6 September: the institutional story changes its defence

The DPO's Article 16 refusal is perhaps even more revealing than the SIMT outcome because it articulates a different kind of continuity.

The institution does not need to prove that every historical opinion remains clinically correct today.

Instead, it says in substance that the historical opinion remains an accurate record because it accurately records what the clinician believed at that time.

That is a legitimate conceptual distinction in record-keeping.

But it is also the precise point of collision with the patient's project.

The patient is not only asking whether the sentence was genuinely written in 2017.

He is asking whether a historical representation can remain **operationally unqualified** after later evidence has materially changed its meaning.

The institutional position locates accuracy in historical provenance:

> this was the opinion then.

The patient locates accuracy partly in present informational effect:

> if this continues to govern now, the later contradiction must travel with it.

This is no longer just a dispute about diagnosis.

It is a dispute about what a medical record *is*.

---

## 17. 29 September: the patient learns that sometimes the inaccurate line must stay

A later GDPR intervention shows how far the patient's thinking has developed.

He identifies a statement that he did not want contact with clinicians and regards it as false.

But he does **not** ask for deletion.

He expressly says he wants the line preserved, flagged as disputed and restricted because he considers it probative evidence.

That behaviour is extremely revealing.

The patient's project is no longer "make every document agree with me."

He has learned to distinguish correction from preservation.

An inaccurate statement may itself need to survive because its existence proves something about the institutional record-making process.

What he wants restricted is not the existence of history.

It is the ability of disputed history to continue governing without qualification.

That distinction strengthens the interpretation of his earlier Article 16 activity: the project is not simple erasure of an unwanted past. It is control over the evidential meaning and operational use of that past.

---

## 18. November: the patient's search breaks through the hospital walls

By November, the patient has concluded that the hospital may not be the only institutional locus capable of testing the 2017 Protest.

He turns to the University.

The significance is concrete.

If four trainees were present in a supervised bedside teaching interaction, then their presence may have generated records outside the hospital's ordinary clinical file: allocations, logbooks, case discussions, teaching notes, supervisory records or academic governance material.

The combined Articles 15, 16 and 18 request to the University therefore performs three linked functions:

- **find the traces;**
- **correct any inherited inaccurate teaching representation;**
- **stop further educational use while accuracy is unresolved.**

This is the patient attempting to recover the omitted Protest from the real world around the record.

He is no longer dependent upon asking the hospital that holds the authoritative discharge history whether that history is complete.

He is asking another possible controller whether the event left an independent institutional shadow.

That is a profound change from the patient who wrote on 19 March asking the hospital itself to investigate.

---

## 19. November appointments: the patient sees the old story still acting on his body

The appointment correspondence matters because it prevents the GDPR story from becoming abstract.

The patient receives another appointment under the same treating pathway and objects that disputed data is continuing to generate an unsafe route.

Whether every legal proposition in that objection is ultimately accepted is a separate question.

The behavioural fact is clear:

for the patient, the old information state is not archival.

It still determines where he is sent and under whom he is expected to receive care.

That is why restriction becomes clinically meaningful to him.

The patient is not fighting for the aesthetic cleanliness of a database.

He is trying to stop the old story from continuing to allocate his future.

---

## 20. December 2025–January 2026: the patient gets the strongest possible test of the "before and after" problem

The later dystonia diagnosis changes the argument again.

The patient now possesses external clinical information that, in his understanding, materially changes the diagnostic landscape.

Importantly, his January re-engagement does not need to prove that every earlier clinician acted negligently.

The question becomes narrower:

**what should the present record and present governance position do with materially different later information?**

This is the before-and-after problem in its cleanest form.

The patient says, in effect:

> Even if you insist the historical record accurately records what was thought then, you now know something different. What changes now? What gets qualified? What remains restricted? Who is told?

The institution's January answer is therefore extraordinarily important.

QPS states that updated diagnoses can supersede earlier diagnoses clinically but do not thereby invalidate the earlier governance process, require retrospective amendment of historical records, or require reopening the previous determination.

The institution has now articulated the temporal rule that the patient has been fighting since March:

**the present may change without the institutional past being reconstructed.**

That is the clearest continuation of the original story in the post-clinical documents.

---

## 21. 2026 Podiatry access: the patient follows another surviving branch of 2017

The later Podiatry SAR completes another part of the reconstruction.

The original 2017 story placed Podiatry beside the feet as a practical disposition.

Years later, when the patient understands that this pathway itself may be load-bearing, he follows the data to the service that actually held the Podiatry/Chiropody records.

He asks for the referrals, referral-processing material, clinical notes, appointments, waiting lists, closure or discharge records, and communications with Neurology and primary care.

Again the purpose is historical but the legal mechanism is present-day.

He is trying to answer a question the 2017 discharge narrative cannot answer by itself:

**what did Podiatry actually receive, what was it asked to do, what did it do, and what did it communicate back?**

When he considers the response incomplete, he escalates that access issue to the DPC.

The patient is still doing in 2026 what began in March 2025: trying to reconstruct the process that produced the story under which he had lived.

But by now he knows where to look.

---

## 22. What the patient voice shows that the finished repository theory cannot show by itself

The repository now possesses a mature theory: Protest, omission, dependency, recursive validation, restriction.

There is a danger in reading that mature theory backwards and imagining that the patient understood it all from the beginning.

The dated documents show otherwise.

That is one of their strongest evidential features.

The patient develops in stages.

### March

He knows the medication worked and asks the hospital to explain why the possibility was not explored earlier.

### April

He begins preserving his rebuttal because institutional closure has created another record he does not trust to stand alone.

### May

He challenges the handling process while still trying to escape a treatment relationship he no longer experiences as safe.

### June

He discovers accuracy as a means of forcing a before-and-after into the record.

### July

He recognises that the missing Protest may be recoverable through the four people allegedly present and the institutional traces around them.

### August

He recognises that disputed history can generate new authoritative outputs and therefore seeks restriction.

### September

He distinguishes correction from preservation and wants an allegedly false statement retained as evidence but prevented from governing.

### November

He moves beyond the hospital to the University and treats the teaching interaction as a second-controller problem.

### January 2026

He tests whether genuinely new diagnostic information can make the institution alter its historical governance position.

### July–August 2026

He follows the Podiatry branch into another HSE data environment and asks what that route actually contained.

This is not the behaviour of a static complainant repeating one accusation.

It is the behaviour of someone discovering the architecture of his own case while the institutions around him continue to generate documents.

---

## 23. What the institutional voice shows across the same period

The institutional documents also develop, but differently.

The language changes while the structural function remains comparatively stable.

### Clinical continuity

The medication response is placed inside an existing story of known dopamine responsiveness and deliberate treatment restraint.

### Jurisdictional continuity

The complaint is classified through clinical judgment and initially answered through the treating clinician.

### Governance continuity

The matter is escalated to incident review, but the resulting conclusion is still that the care was appropriate and that there was no deficit.

### Data continuity

Historical professional opinion is treated as capable of remaining factually accurate because it accurately records the opinion held at the time.

### Temporal continuity

Later diagnostic change may alter the clinical position prospectively without requiring earlier governance conclusions to be reopened.

These are not identical arguments.

But each one protects a different layer of the same institutional past from being destabilised by later contradiction.

That is why the repository should not reduce the story to "the hospital kept saying the same thing."

It did something more sophisticated.

**It changed the level at which continuity was defended.**

First clinically.

Then procedurally.

Then through governance.

Then through data-protection reasoning.

Then through an explicit distinction between present clinical supersession and historical institutional validity.

## 24. The post-March documents therefore operate almost like a live experiment

The clinical record before March 2025 suggests a mechanism:

- uncertainty is recorded;
- a practical course is selected;
- the patient's alleged challenge is absent from the authoritative synthesis;
- later clinicians inherit the completed account;
- contradictory neurological evidence produces treatment changes but no visible foundational reconstruction.

After March 2025 the patient explicitly challenges that history.

We can then watch what the institution does.

The observed post-March behaviour is strikingly consistent with the earlier mechanism:

- contradiction changes what happens next;
- the historical account remains substantially intact;
- new processes are created;
- each process produces another document;
- later documents rely on earlier information states;
- new clinical information is permitted prospective significance without necessarily acquiring retrospective force.

This does **not** prove that the 2017 Protest occurred or that it was knowingly omitted.

It does something different.

It shows that the system observed after notice behaves in a way that is compatible with the repository's model of how the earlier record could have acquired and retained authority.

That is important behavioural evidence about the institution.

---

## 25. The patient's behaviour is also evidence, but of a different kind

The patient's evolving correspondence cannot prove the missing 2017 conversation merely by becoming detailed.

But it is relevant to several narrower questions.

First, it shows **acquisition of knowledge over time**.

The mature omission theory is absent from the first complaint and emerges only after record access, comparison and institutional response.

Second, it shows **consistency of the underlying grievance despite development of the theory**.

From the beginning, the problem is the same: the feet, the prolonged suffering, the medication response, and the belief that the earlier course had not been properly reconsidered.

Third, it shows **why the patient increasingly cared about records**.

He did not begin as a data-protection litigant. He became one when he concluded that the record was controlling care, complaint handling and the institutional meaning of the past.

Fourth, it shows **preservation behaviour**.

He repeatedly asks that objections, disputed statements and underlying records be retained. That behaviour is consistent with a person trying to prevent later institutional summaries from becoming the only surviving account.

Fifth, it shows **movement toward external verification**.

The trajectory goes from asking the hospital to investigate, to asking the DPO to rectify, to asking for witness-related data, to approaching the University, to following Podiatry, to placing the sequence before the regulator.

The direction of travel is away from assertion and toward attempted corroboration.

That does not establish that corroboration will ultimately succeed.

It is nevertheless important to the story of what the patient was doing.

---

## 26. The deepest conflict is temporal

By 2026, the patient and institution are not simply disagreeing about a diagnosis.

They are disagreeing about **how later knowledge is allowed to act upon earlier institutional truth**.

The patient's position becomes:

> If later evidence materially changes what the old information means, the present record must carry that change backward far enough to prevent the old representation from continuing to govern unqualified.

The institutional position becomes:

> Earlier records and governance decisions can remain valid as records of what was known, believed or decided at the time, even where later clinical information supersedes them prospectively.

That is the core dispute running through Article 16, Article 18, SIMT and the January 2026 correspondence.

The patient wants a **before and after**.

The institution repeatedly preserves a **then and now**.

Those sound similar, but they are not.

A before-and-after structure allows the later event to change the meaning of what came before.

A then-and-now structure allows the two states to coexist while leaving the historical state institutionally insulated.

That is why the patient's GDPR campaign becomes so persistent.

He is trying to force the record to acknowledge not merely that circumstances changed, but that the later change may alter what the earlier history is permitted to mean in the present.

---

## 27. The case being told by the repository is therefore a story of two forms of memory

The institution remembers through documents.

The patient remembers through documents **and through lived continuity**.

In 2017, if his evidence is accepted, he told the institution that the feet remained unresolved.

The institutional history did not preserve that objection.

The patient's body did.

The feet continued.

The treatment responses continued.

The suffering continued.

The crisis language entered the record.

Then in March 2025 a tablet produced a result that caused the patient to look backward.

From that moment, he began trying to convert lived memory back into institutional memory.

The complaint was the first attempt.

Rectification was another.

The teaching SAR attempted to find the witnesses around the missing event.

Restriction attempted to stop the disputed historical state from producing more institutional descendants.

The University request attempted to escape the limits of the hospital archive.

The Podiatry SAR attempted to reconstruct another branch of the original disposition.

The DPC file became an external memory of the dispute itself.

That is the thread connecting the entire post-March correspondence.

---

## Final proposition

The strongest way to read the post-2025 documents is not as a pile of complaints and GDPR exercises.

They are a live record of **discovery**.

The patient begins with one fact: the tablet worked.

He asks the institution why.

The institution answers from the continuity of the existing history.

That answer causes the patient to examine the history more closely.

He begins preserving his disagreement.

He discovers that complaint handling can itself create authoritative history.

He turns to accuracy.

He identifies the missing Protest as potentially load-bearing.

He remembers that four trainees were there.

He uses access rights to try to recover them.

He realises the disputed old story is still being used to generate present decisions.

He invokes restriction.

He learns that an inaccurate statement can itself be evidence and therefore asks that it be preserved rather than erased.

He moves outside the hospital to the University.

He later follows Podiatry into another data environment.

And when a later dystonia diagnosis gives him the clearest possible test of whether new knowledge can alter the institutional past, the institution answers that the new diagnosis may supersede the old one clinically without requiring the old governance conclusions to be reopened.

That is the story now visible in the repository.

The institution's story becomes progressively more formal.

The patient's story becomes progressively more informed.

The institution accumulates authoritative documents.

The patient accumulates contradictions, requests, preservation notices and external routes by which those documents might finally be tested.

The repository's task is therefore not merely to prove that something was missing in 2017.

It is to show **what happened when the missing thing eventually became visible to the person who had lived inside its consequences**.

From March 2025 onward, the case stops being only a retrospective reconstruction.

It becomes observable institutional behaviour.

And because the patient's own documents are dated, sequential and increasingly sophisticated, we can watch him understand the case at the same time that we watch the institution continue to tell its own.

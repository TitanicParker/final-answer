# The Formal Complaint of 19 March 2025: Notice Before Full Discovery

The formal complaint submitted on 19 March 2025 is a contemporaneous patient-origin document produced nine days after Liam’s final consultation with the treating neurologist and five days after he first experienced profound relief from nighttime Sinemet CR. It should be preserved as evidence of what Liam understood, suspected and communicated at that particular moment—not treated as a complete pleading of the case later disclosed by the records.

The complaint identifies its subject as the clinical handling of Liam’s case by Galway East Medical Practice and the Neurology Department at Galway University Hospitals between 2017 and 2025. Its stated concerns are diagnostic anchoring, bias and failure to reassess. It describes the principal injury as prolonged, disabling foot pain which had persisted despite years of clinical engagement.

The temporal circumstances are essential. On 10 March 2025, Liam had described “murder in the feet,” said that his first 150 footsteps were “torture,” and told the neurologist, “I won’t live with it.” Sinemet CR was prescribed that day. Liam collected it on 13 March and first woke with the resulting relief on 14 March. The complaint followed on 19 March. It was therefore written in the immediate aftermath of a grave clinical encounter and a treatment response which had radically altered Liam’s understanding of his history.

The complaint states that the turning point occurred when Sinemet CR produced “immediate and sustained relief from two decades of disabling foot pain.” It characterises that response as effectively disproving the prior working diagnosis and suggesting that the condition had been dopamine-responsive throughout. In strict clinical terms, medication response alone may not conclusively exclude every peripheral contribution. Evidentially, however, the complaint unmistakably placed the hospital on notice that a targeted dopaminergic intervention had produced relief of sufficient magnitude to require retrospective examination of the preceding course.

The central question posed was:

> “Why was this possibility not explored earlier, given the magnitude of harm endured?”

That was not merely a request for an explanation of the current prescription. It was an express demand that the institution look backwards. The hospital was being asked to determine whether the profound response required reassessment of the diagnoses, classifications and treatment decisions under which the foot pain had been managed since 2017.

The complaint expressly alleged diagnostic anchoring. Liam believed that an early attribution of the pain to peripheral neuropathy, possibly alcohol-related, had become fixed and had followed him through later referrals and specialist reviews. He further alleged that deterioration, continuing pain and functional impairment had not produced a sufficiently fresh neurological inquiry into Parkinson’s-related pain or dopamine-responsive conditions.

The accuracy of every clinical formulation used by Liam on 19 March is not the decisive evidential question. He acknowledged within the complaint that he did not possess all internal notes, referrals and correspondence. He was describing the course as it appeared to the patient before full documentary disclosure. If his description of the precise diagnostic label was incomplete, the requested independent review was the mechanism by which the institution was supposed to determine what had actually occurred.

The complaint also raised “Potential Hierarchical Influence.” Liam stated that he had reason to believe senior clinical influence had advised caution against particular treatments and that, combined with referral bias, this may have closed viable therapeutic trials. This was a measured but material notice of the substance later reflected more clearly in the records: the senior-backed desire to hold back levodopa and the governing strategy of withholding ordinary dopaminergic treatment for as long as possible.

Importantly, the complaint did not allege malice, concealment or a coordinated course of misconduct. It expressly stated:

> “I make no presumptions about individual intent or malice.”

Liam identified his concern as systemic: that diagnostic framing, confirmation bias and deference to hierarchy may have overridden patient experience and clinical curiosity. The complaint was therefore restrained in both tone and allegation. It asked the hospital to investigate rather than pretending that the patient already possessed the internal evidence required to make definitive findings.

The harms identified were physical, psychological and institutional. The physical harm was described as prolonged, preventable pain. The psychological harm included fear of movement, cumulative distress and erosion of resilience. The institutional injury was loss of trust in the capacity of the clinical system to correct itself. These descriptions were consistent with the pre-existing clinical record, which had documented pain that psychologically consumed Liam, a “psychological mountain” associated with walking, a life which had become “a lot smaller,” and foot pain “causing death wish.”

The complaint did not, however, disclose the full case now under examination. It did not identify the 10 July 2017 Protest, because Liam did not yet understand that its absence from the discharge synthesis had acquired institutional significance. It did not allege that the Protest had been knowingly omitted. It did not reconstruct how a contested discharge course became an apparently accepted foundation. It did not identify the documentary relationship between the unresolved feet, the dystonic-looking gait, the PRKN hypothesis, Professor Counihan’s knowledge, the early Procyclidine response and the later treatment history.

Nor did it allege that the continuity of the subsequent record was genealogically dependent upon the 2017 synthesis. That architecture became visible only through later access to and comparison of the records and through the hospital’s own reliance upon the resulting continuity when answering the complaint. The complaint therefore evidences knowledge of injury, treatment response and a suspected failure to reassess. It does not, by itself, establish that Liam had discovered the originating omission, its possible motive, or its capacity to shape the later documentary environment.

The relief sought confirms the distinction. Liam requested an independent review of the clinical reasoning applied between 2017 and 2025; full disclosure of the records and internal communications; a written explanation of how the outcome had occurred; and preservation of all relevant material pending his review. He was asking the institution to supply the information necessary to determine the case. He was not representing that he already possessed it.

Those requests placed QPS and the hospital on notice of a potential longitudinal systems failure. The institution was asked to test whether early framing had persisted, whether later evidence had triggered genuine reassessment, whether senior influence had restricted viable therapeutic inquiry, and whether prolonged pain had been avoidable. The hospital was also expressly asked to preserve and disclose the material required for an independent answer.

The document’s tone is probative. Liam said that he wrote with respect for the complexity of clinical decision-making and sought accountability, transparency and learning. He described himself as acting not from anger but from humility and a genuine need for understanding. His stated objective was not confrontation but protection of future patients. This was not the language of a complainant presenting a completed accusation. It was the language of a severely affected patient asking the institution to investigate an outcome he had only just begun to comprehend.

Some expressions within the complaint require later evidential refinement. The statement that Liam “independently trialed” Sinemet CR should be read alongside the fact that the medication had been prescribed by the neurologist on 10 March. The statement that dopamine responsiveness disproved the prior diagnosis should be formulated more carefully as evidence materially impeaching the sufficiency of the inherited explanation. Likewise, the allegation that no dopaminergic responsiveness had been tested must now be reconciled with the recorded Procyclidine and Sinemet history. These refinements do not diminish the notice supplied. They demonstrate why an independent review of the complete record was requested.

The complaint is therefore best characterised as a notice-and-discovery document. It records the immediate consequences of profound treatment response, identifies a suspected longitudinal failure, describes serious harm, raises the possibility of hierarchical treatment restraint, and asks the hospital to disclose and independently examine the underlying evidence. It is close to the present case in injury and mechanism, but it does not yet contain the impeached-hinge architecture later revealed through documentary reconstruction.

Its strongest evidential formulation is:

> The formal complaint of 19 March 2025 demonstrates that Liam had discovered profound dopaminergic relief and suspected that diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess and hierarchical treatment restraint had contributed to prolonged harm. It simultaneously demonstrates that he lacked the internal records necessary to know how that result had been produced. He therefore requested independent review, disclosure, explanation and preservation. The later case concerning the omitted Protest and the dependent documentary framework was not concealed inside the complaint; it emerged when the institution answered that request through reliance upon the very continuity whose independence required investigation.

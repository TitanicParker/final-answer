# The Spoken Record: The Evidence the Clinical Notes Do Not Exhaust

The institutional record is indispensable, but it is not the whole evidential history. Some of the most consequential events in this case occurred in speech: what the neurologist said after the Complex Case Meeting, what the patient said when he protested against discharge, what he was told about DBS, and what he communicated about the effect of treatment upon his feet. These matters must be assessed according to their evidential source, but they cannot properly be excluded merely because the institution did not record them.

The patient recalls that, after the Complex Case Meeting in July 2017, the neurologist approached him and said:

> “My boss told me not to give you the good stuff.”

That is patient testimony concerning words spoken directly to him. It is not presently a quotation preserved in a native clinical record. Nevertheless, its substance is materially consistent with the written record. The discharge documentation attributes the requirement for “the greatest of care” to Professor Counihan and Neurology colleagues, adopts a policy of avoiding ordinary dopaminergic treatment for as long as possible, and later states:

> “Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you’d advise otherwise?”

The patient’s recollection therefore does not stand alone as an improbable or extraneous assertion. The institutional documents independently establish the senior origin and practical effect of the restraint described to him. The precise spoken words remain a matter for his evidence; the surrounding decision architecture is documented.

The patient also recalls protesting against discharge in terms which captured the clinical defect with unusual precision:

> “Do you mean to tell me you’re going to bring me in here, give me the full NCT, and send me out on bald tyres?”

The metaphor was not incidental. The patient had presented through his GP with painful feet and a gait which he himself attributed to that pain. The referral placed the feet at the beginning of the neurological inquiry. The admission recorded corns, calluses, fallen arches, continuing foot problems and a gait suggestive of dystonic posture. Yet the relationship between the feet and the neurological syndrome was left “Not clear,” without any visible investigation capable of explaining why it remained unclear.

The Protest was therefore not an objection to an exhaustive investigation producing an uncertain result. On the patient’s account, it exposed that the principal weight-bearing problem which had brought him into care had been recorded at entry but had remained clinically stationary. The institution had examined the engine while leaving unresolved the point at which the patient’s body met the ground.

The absence of the Protest from the discharge synthesis does not prove that it did not occur. It creates the evidential issue. The document preserved extensive descriptions of the patient’s understanding, questions and supposed acceptance. It incorporated post-discharge confirmation from the DaTSCAN. It invoked senior authority and enclosed specialist genetic material. But it did not preserve the patient’s contemporaneous challenge to the adequacy of the foot disposition.

The result was not merely an incomplete transcript. The omission concealed that a choice had been presented. The clinicians could have delayed discharge, examined the feet more closely, obtained movement-disorder input, preserved dystonia as an active issue, or considered a cautious diagnostic treatment trial. Alternatively, they could proceed with the existing senior-backed course. The record shows the course which prevailed, but not the patient’s attempt to force reconsideration of it.

Later quotations illuminate what followed. When Procyclidine was withdrawn, the patient reported:

> “My feet are restless and my walk is falling apart.”

He also described the improvement produced by treatment as:

> “Living in a miracle.”

Those reports gave Neurology early notice that the gait and feet were neurologically modifiable. For the patient, this response was reassuring: he had told the clinicians what happened, and he reasonably believed that they now understood it. He did not yet appreciate that the original partition could remain embedded in the management history despite the treatment response.

The signed clinic letter of 27 October 2021 contains another categorical statement:

> “The DBS is only ever for the tremor element. It will not help with anything else.”

Unlike the two 2017 conversations, this statement is preserved in the neurologist’s own document. Its importance is not confined to whether it accurately described the full therapeutic indications for DBS. It informed the patient’s understanding of what future treatment could offer him. When another specialist told him in December 2025 that he would be a very good DBS candidate, the patient answered:

> “No, sure, that’s only ever for tremor.”

That repetition is evidence of reliance. The earlier sentence had travelled with him for more than four years. It had become part of the boundary within which he understood his own treatment possibilities.

The record then documents an escalating human consequence. The foot pain “psychologically consumes him.” A third walk becomes “a psychological mountain.” His life becomes “a lot smaller.” In 2023, the GP reports that extreme burning pain is “causing death wish” and warns:

> “Despite his facade, I don’t think he is doing well. I’d be grateful for your review before we get into trouble here.”

On 10 March 2025, the patient describes “murder in the feet,” says that the first 150 footsteps are “torture,” and states:

> “I won’t live with it.”

These are not retrospective expressions created after a dispute arose. They are successive warnings preserved inside the clinical record. They demonstrate the continuity and gravity of the patient’s experience before the formal complaint.

The spoken evidence, documentary quotations and treatment history must therefore be considered together. The patient’s recollections should be identified as recollections; they should not be misrepresented as institutional entries. His observations of the neurologist’s demeanour should similarly be expressed as observations: the neurologist appeared nervous and did not make or maintain eye contact. The patient can prove what he saw; the court may determine what, if anything, should be inferred from it.

But evidential discipline does not require the patient to surrender everything the institution failed to record. His testimony is evidence. Its credibility can be tested against chronology, contemporaneous documents, subsequent conduct, inherent probability and independent documentary echoes.

The resulting formulation is:

> The written record establishes the senior-backed restraint, the unresolved feet, the early neurological treatment response, the categorical DBS advice, the later crisis language and the eventual profound relief. The patient’s testimony supplies the missing conversations through which those events were experienced and challenged. Far from contradicting the documentary history, the spoken account explains why the documentary history took the shape it did—and what disappeared when the Protest was omitted.

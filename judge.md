Judge, this is not a case in which the plaintiff asks the Court to find that every diagnosis made after July 2017 was wrong or that every clinician who subsequently treated him acted improperly. The case concerns one disputed event at the point of discharge, the accuracy of the account created after that event, and the consequences of later decisions being made upon that account.

When the plaintiff was admitted, Parkinsonism was identified. What had not been resolved was whether his dominant disabling foot and lower-limb problem formed part of that neurological syndrome. The contemporaneous record described the relationship as “Not clear” and the lower-limb posture as appearing dystonic. Notwithstanding that uncertainty, the feet were placed upon a Podiatry pathway and dopaminergic treatment was to be withheld.

The plaintiff’s evidence is that he objected before discharge. His objection was not that he did not have Parkinsonism. It was that the principal disability for which he had sought help remained unexplained and that the proposed disposition did not answer it. Whether that Protest occurred, who heard it and whether the author of the discharge account knew of it are factual matters for determination on the evidence.

The discharge account was completed after the alleged Protest. It recorded the treatment rationale, senior clinical involvement, Podiatry, the patient’s “good understanding” and an apparently settled plan. It later incorporated the confirmatory DaTSCAN result. It did not record that the plaintiff had challenged the adequacy of the foot disposition before discharge.

The plaintiff’s case is that, if the Court accepts the Protest occurred and was known to the author, its omission was material. The completed document did not merely fail to reproduce every conversation. It omitted the one conversation which showed that an unresolved clinical transition had been contested before it became the basis of future care.

The plaintiff does not have to establish that every subsequent clinician shared any purpose associated with that omission. His case is that those clinicians inherited an apparently settled clinical history. To the extent that later decisions depended upon the propositions that the feet had been adequately separated, that Podiatry constituted a sufficient disposition or that the patient had accepted the plan, repetition of those propositions did not independently prove them. They continued to derive from the disputed account.

The later records show that the foot problem did not disappear. It repeatedly re-entered neurological care, exhibited medication-related variation and was considered in connection with toe-curling, possible dystonia and OFF-related symptoms. Parkinson medication was ultimately recorded as relieving the feet, and Sinemet CR, introduced specifically in response to the severe morning problem, produced “profound relief.” The plaintiff does not rely upon that later response as automatic proof of what treatment was legally required in 2017. He relies upon it as evidence that the unresolved question remained material and repeatedly called for reconsideration.

The consequences were serious. In 2023, an urgent referral recorded that the bilateral foot and lower-leg suffering was extreme and “causing death wish.” By 2025, the experience was described in the neurological record as “murder in the feet,” “torture” and something the plaintiff “won’t live with.” Those statements require careful interpretation and do not alone prove continuous suicidal intent. They do, however, provide contemporaneous evidence of the extraordinary severity of the suffering.

When the plaintiff complained, the response relied upon the continuity of the clinical record. It stated that Sinemet responsiveness had long been understood, that treatment restraint had been deliberate and that the eventual profound response was consistent with the established approach. The plaintiff’s complaint is that the same continuity was not used to return to the unresolved 2017 question or to confront the significance of the recorded death wish.

The issue for the Court is therefore not whether continuity existed. It plainly did. The issue is what that continuity proves. The defendant may say it demonstrates a careful and consistent treatment strategy. The plaintiff says that, insofar as it depended upon an incomplete account of the disputed discharge transition, it demonstrates the reach of that account rather than independently validating it.

Ultimately, the plaintiff must prove each necessary link on the balance of probabilities. He must prove the Protest, the responsible person’s knowledge of it, its materiality, the effect of its omission upon the record received by later clinicians, the decisions that materially depended upon that record, and—through appropriate expert evidence—the difference that accurate recording and proper reconsideration would probably have made.

Put shortly, the plaintiff’s case is that a contested and unresolved clinical decision was recorded as settled; the settled account became the premise of later care; later developments were managed without visibly reopening that premise; the patient suffered gravely within the resulting course; and the continuity created by that course was ultimately relied upon to defend its own foundation.

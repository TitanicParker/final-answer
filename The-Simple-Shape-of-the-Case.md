# The Simple Shape of the Case

## What happened, why it matters, and what still has to be proved

This case is difficult because it covers many years, many records and several different hospital processes. But the central point is simple.

Liam went into care with a serious problem involving his feet, pain and walking. Parkinsonism was identified. The feet were recorded, but their relationship to the neurological condition was not resolved. Liam says he protested before discharge because the problem that brought him into care had not been dealt with. That Protest does not appear in the completed discharge account. The account instead carried a settled plan into the future.

Over the following years, the feet repeatedly behaved like a neurological problem. Neurological medication changed them. Lack of medication made them worse. Later levodopa treatment brought major relief. Care changed in response to these facts, but the original account was not visibly rebuilt from the beginning.

When Liam complained in March 2025, the hospital relied on the continuity of the care and the existing record. It did not first establish whether the record at the start of that continuity was complete. The same history then passed into complaint handling, data protection and governance review.

That is the simple shape of the case.

It is not necessary to say that every later clinician acted badly. It is not necessary to say that Parkinsonism was wrongly identified. It is not necessary to allege a conspiracy lasting eight years.

The question is whether an unresolved and contested decision became an apparently settled foundation, and whether the future built on that foundation was later used to defend it.

## 1. Why Liam entered care

The feet were not a late complaint added to an established Parkinson's case.

The available GP history shows that foot pain was central when Liam first sought help in 2017. The referral of 29 June 2017 begins with that problem. It records that Liam had presented in April with pain in his feet and that he believed the pain was affecting the way he walked.

The referral contained other neurological concerns. Those concerns properly required neurological investigation. But the feet, pain and gait were part of the same presenting problem. They were not background decoration.

This matters because later records can make the case look as though Parkinson's disease came first and the feet were a separate difficulty considered afterwards. The chronology is the other way around. The painful feet and altered walking were already present when the neurological relationship began.

## 2. What the admission established

Liam was admitted in July 2017. The neurological work was important. Parkinsonism was identified on clinical grounds and was later supported by an abnormal DaTSCAN.

The admission also recorded the feet. The records refer to corns, calluses, fallen arches, pes planus and hammer toes. They refer to a long history of Podiatry. Most importantly, the examination describes the walking or foot position as looking somewhat like a dystonic posture and makes clear that it was not ordinary foot drop.

The discharge material says that the relationship between the feet and the neurological condition was “Not clear.”

“Not clear” can mean two very different things.

It can mean that a problem was carefully investigated but medicine could not give a firm answer.

It can also mean that the question was noticed but was not taken far enough to produce an answer.

The present record must be examined to decide which meaning applies here.

There is no visible chain of foot-focused neurological reasoning after the initial examination. There is no clear record of repeated examination, a movement-disorders assessment of the foot posture, a defined test of whether the feet changed with neurological treatment, or a named clinician made responsible for resolving the relationship. Listing structural features is not the same thing as explaining what caused the pain and altered gait.

The real question is therefore not merely why the relationship remained unclear after eight days. It is what was actually done during those eight days to make it clear.

## 3. The treatment limit

The admission did not take place in an open treatment field.

The records show a strong wish to avoid ordinary dopaminergic treatment for as long as possible. Senior advice was invoked. Professor Timothy Counihan was named. A later formulation described the position as shared by the treating neurologist and “Tim.”

There may have been sound reasons for caution. Liam was young. The clinicians were concerned about the long future of treatment, later motor complications and the consequences of excessive medication. Those concerns should not be dismissed.

But caution is not the same thing as a complete ban on learning.

If a small, supervised treatment trial could have helped show whether the feet were neurologically modifiable, then the decision not to use that tool needs a patient-specific explanation. The issue is not simply whether long-term levodopa should have started in July 2017. The issue is whether the treatment policy prevented the team from testing the problem that remained unclear.

That question is especially important because the discharge package included a Counihan-authored article dealing with young-onset PRKN disease, dystonia and strong levodopa response. The same package recorded a dystonic-looking foot or lower-limb posture and considered PRKN. Yet the patient-specific reasoning did not visibly join those points together.

## 4. Podiatry was not new

The record also shows that Liam had already been attending Podiatry for years. The feet remained a problem despite that history.

During the admission, a Podiatry route appeared again. The GP made a Podiatry request on 6 July using Parkinson's as the condition. The later discharge account also asked for Podiatry.

Podiatry was not necessarily wrong. Structural pressure, callus and foot shape can require Podiatry even where a neurological condition contributes to the loading or posture.

But a referral is not an answer to the causal question. It does not explain whether dystonia, rigidity, abnormal loading, structural change and pain were connected. It does not show that the dominant problem had been investigated neurologically. And because Liam had already had Podiatry, another referral did not by itself represent clear forward movement.

## 5. The Protest

Liam says that before discharge he objected directly.

He recalls saying, in substance, that the hospital had given him the full NCT but was sending him out on bald tyres. The meaning was plain: many tests had been done, but the part of his body that met the ground and caused the practical disability had not been dealt with.

He says four trainees were present and that one of them went to get the neurologist.

This is presently patient evidence. The surviving record does not itself prove that the conversation happened. The witnesses, rotas, teaching arrangements and any other surviving traces must be investigated.

But the content of the reported Protest fits the documented position at the time. The feet were central to presentation. They remained a problem. A dystonic-looking posture had been observed. The relationship was still described as unclear. Podiatry was being used again. Discharge was about to occur.

The Protest was not a demand that medicine provide certainty where certainty was impossible. It was a complaint that the principal problem had been recorded at the start and then left standing still while the rest of the neurological work moved forward.

That distinction matters.

## 6. The omission

The completed discharge account does not record the Protest.

Absence alone does not prove a knowing omission. Medical records do not contain every conversation. A court or investigator would need evidence that the author knew of the Protest, understood its importance and completed the account without it.

But this was not an ordinary passing comment. If Liam's account is true, the Protest challenged discharge, the treatment limit and the use of Podiatry as the practical answer to an unresolved problem.

The discharge document was meant to consolidate the admission, discussions and forward plan. It recorded that Liam had good understanding. It preserved the senior-backed treatment position. It incorporated the plan as set out in the narrative. It was capable of receiving later confirmatory information: the post-discharge DaTSCAN result was added as expected confirmation.

The document therefore included confirmation and the appearance of understanding, but not the alleged disagreement.

If that was a knowing choice, the immediate effect was important. A contested plan entered the future as an apparently sufficient and accepted plan.

That is the hinge of the case.

## 7. What motive would mean

Motive should not be spread across the whole later history.

The narrow question is why the Protest, if known and understood, was not included in the completed account.

One possible reason is that recording it would have required the team to stop and reconsider. It might have required another examination, a senior review, a clearer explanation of what had and had not been investigated, a defined review plan, or a delay in discharge.

That possibility is not yet a proved fact. It is a matter for evidence.

No wider plan is needed to explain what followed. Later clinicians could act honestly and reasonably on the record given to them. If that record made the original position look complete and uncontested, it would shape the questions they asked without their knowing that anything material was missing.

Motive, if proved, belongs at the omission. Dependency explains the future.

## 8. How the record could shape later care

A discharge account does more than describe the past. It prepares the next clinician's starting point.

If the account said that the feet were unresolved, possibly dystonic and disputed by the patient, later clinicians would receive an open question. New medication responses would naturally be considered as evidence about that question.

If the account instead presented a careful Parkinson's assessment, a senior-backed treatment plan, good patient understanding and a Podiatry pathway, later clinicians would receive a case that appeared settled at its foundation.

They might still treat the feet. They might change medication. They might consider dystonia. But they would be less likely to ask whether the original separation of the feet from the neurological condition had been wrong or incomplete.

That is the difference between treating a later symptom and reopening an earlier decision.

## 9. What happened in 2018

The early Procyclidine history is important.

The records indicate marked benefit from this neurological medication. When it was withdrawn, tension, gait difficulty and foot symptoms returned or worsened. Liam reported this to the clinical team.

From Liam's point of view, he had told Neurology that the feet were neurologically modifiable. He had reason to believe the information was now in the right hands. He did not know that the original management structure might continue to treat the feet as separate or unresolved.

This helps explain why he did not immediately investigate the absence of the Protest from the discharge account. He did not expect every conversation to be written there. He did not yet understand that the absence might have changed the meaning of the future record. The medication response appeared to have supplied the missing information directly to the treating team.

The legal importance is not that Procyclidine proves what the final diagnosis should have been. It is that a neurological intervention changed the disputed problem early, and the supplied record does not visibly show that this result caused a reconstruction of the original foot decision.

## 10. The feet kept returning

Across the later record, the feet did not remain quietly inside a simple structural or Podiatry explanation.

The records describe foot movement as parkinsonian. They describe the feet taking time to warm up. They record severe pain, shuffling and reduced mobility when medication had not been taken. They consider OFF symptoms and possible dystonia. They change Parkinson's medication in response.

These are not all the same fact, and none should be forced into a diagnosis without expert opinion. But together they show repeated neurological relevance.

The pattern is that later evidence was often strong enough to change treatment going forward, but not visibly strong enough to change the account of what the feet meant at the beginning.

That is the one-way movement at the centre of the case.

## 11. The harm became severe

The later history is not a dry disagreement about classification.

The records describe prolonged and severe pain, impaired walking and major psychological distress. In 2023, the GP used death-wish language and asked for review before matters became worse. In March 2025, Liam described the first steps of the day as murder or torture and said that he would not live with it.

The exact clinical meaning of each statement must be assessed carefully. Suicidal thought, a death wish, despair and a statement made during extreme pain are not automatically identical. The records should be read by the appropriate experts, not reduced to a slogan.

But the danger and suffering cannot be treated as incidental. The treating system was on notice that the foot pain had become psychologically grave.

For Liam, the injury was not only pain. It included the experience of repeatedly reporting the same bodily problem while the official history did not return to the point at which he says he first challenged it.

## 12. March 2025 changed Liam's knowledge

On 10 March 2025, Sinemet CR was prescribed for nighttime or morning difficulty. Liam collected it on 13 March. He reports waking on 14 March with profound relief in his feet.

That response did not by itself prove negligence. Treatment response cannot answer every question about standard of care, diagnosis or causation.

But it changed what Liam knew.

Before the response, he knew he had suffered and that medication affected him. After the response, he had reason to ask whether the long history of foot pain had been understood correctly and whether earlier treatment or testing might have changed his course.

The emotional effect is also understandable. Relief can reveal the scale of what came before. A person may feel gratitude, grief, anger and confusion at the same time. The better the body suddenly feels, the harder it may become to understand why the suffering lasted so long.

## 13. The complaint of 19 March

Nine days after the final clinic and five days after the reported morning of profound relief, Liam made a formal complaint.

The complaint put the hospital on notice of the central clinical grievance: prolonged foot suffering, treatment response, possible diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess and the need for an independent review and disclosure of the underlying records.

It did not yet contain the mature theory of the Protest and omission.

That is important. It shows the growth of Liam's knowledge. He first complained about what the treatment response appeared to reveal. Only later, after gaining access to records and seeing how the hospital defended the history, did the absence of the 2017 Protest become important to him.

The complaint should not therefore be treated as proof that Liam already knew the full present case. It is evidence that he knew there was a serious problem and asked the hospital to investigate it.

## 14. The complaint response

The neurologist's response was not merely another clinic letter. It was written for QPS and was also given to Liam. It therefore had two functions: it answered the patient and supplied the institution with a professional account on which complaint handling could rely.

The response used the long continuity of the record to explain and support the treatment course. It treated later levodopa responsiveness as something already expected within the original strategy.

That may be a possible clinical explanation. But it does not answer the documentary problem.

If the later record developed within the original account, its continuity cannot automatically prove that the original account was complete. Repetition may show inheritance rather than independent agreement.

Before continuity could safely support the response, the hospital needed to ask whether the beginning of that continuity had been properly recorded and independently tested.

The supplied material does not visibly show that work.

## 15. Why the Protest became important later

Liam did not expect his NCT and bald-tyres words to appear in the discharge summary. For years, their absence did not appear to him to be the problem.

He had reported the medication effects. Neurology was treating him. He assumed the team knew what his feet were doing.

The omission became important only when the hospital relied on the completed documentary history to rebut the complaint and close the matter. Liam then had reason to compare what he remembered with the account being used against him.

The Protest mattered because it showed that the plan had not been uncontested at the time. Its absence mattered because the hospital's later defence relied on the appearance of a coherent and accepted course.

This is why knowledge of suffering is not necessarily the same as knowledge of the present claim. Liam always knew he was in pain. He did not always know how the record had organised that pain, what had been omitted, or how the omission might be affecting later care and review.

Questions about legal time limits require Irish legal advice and should be protected urgently. But the chronology of actual knowledge must be stated accurately.

## 16. The move into governance

After the complaint, the case changed form.

The clinical relationship was ending, but the institutional use of the record continued. The matter moved through QPS, complaint handling, incident processes, subject access, rectification, restriction and SIMT review.

At each stage, Liam tried to push the later evidence back into the beginning. He wanted the hospital to ask whether the 2017 foundation had been complete.

The institution was often able to answer a narrower forward-looking question instead. Current care could change. A later diagnosis could be recorded. Data requests could be processed. A complaint could be closed. An incident could be classified.

But the old account remained substantially in place.

This is why the governance history is part of the case rather than an administrative appendix. It shows what the institution did after being told that the foundation itself was disputed.

## 17. The SIMT outcome

The SIMT conclusion of no deficit in care is important, but not because it automatically starts a new clinical-negligence claim or proves wrongdoing.

It is important because it is the institution's own later judgment about the history.

The question is whether SIMT independently rebuilt the case from the original referral, the admission, the foot examination, the treatment limit, the alleged Protest, the discharge document, the early medication response, the later OFF history, the March 2025 relief and the complaint.

If it did that work, the records and reasoning should show it.

If it relied mainly on the clinician's response and the inherited clinical record, then its conclusion may be another descendant of the disputed foundation rather than independent validation of it.

The fact that a later diagnosis or treatment can change prospectively does not answer whether an earlier governance finding should be reconsidered. A system that permits the present to change while protecting the past from review may preserve consistency at the cost of learning.

## 18. What the later record proves—and what it does not

The later record proves that the feet repeatedly mattered to neurological care. It proves that medication changes affected function and pain. It proves that the suffering became severe. It proves that the hospital later relied on continuity when responding to the complaint. It proves that governance processes produced decisions while the historical account remained disputed.

The later record does not, by itself, prove that the Protest happened. It does not prove that anyone knowingly omitted it. It does not prove that earlier levodopa should have been started. It does not prove that all later pain had one cause. It does not prove legal negligence or causation.

Those matters require witnesses, complete records and independent expert evidence.

But the later record cannot fairly be used in only one direction. If it was created inside the 2017 framework, it may show the reach of that framework. It cannot automatically serve as independent proof that the framework was correct.

## 19. What must now be established

The case should be reduced to answerable questions.

First, what investigation of the feet actually occurred during the 2017 admission?

Second, what did the senior consultants know about the feet, pain, dystonic-looking posture and unresolved relationship when treatment advice was given?

Third, what did the Complex Case Meeting decide, and where is its record?

Fourth, did the Protest occur as Liam recalls, who witnessed it and who knew about it before the discharge account was completed?

Fifth, why was the Protest absent while understanding, senior agreement and later confirmation were included?

Sixth, what would a competent movement-disorders neurologist have done in July 2017?

Seventh, what did the Procyclidine, levodopa, OFF and Sinemet CR responses mean clinically?

Eighth, would earlier investigation or treatment probably have reduced the later pain, disability and psychological injury?

Ninth, which later decisions depended on the original discharge account, and which had genuinely independent support?

Tenth, did the complaint and SIMT processes independently test the disputed foundation or merely restate its descendants?

These questions are difficult, but they are ordinary questions of evidence. They can be investigated.

## 20. What this case is not

This is not a claim that every reference to Parkinson's disease was false.

It is not a claim that structural foot problems did not exist.

It is not a claim that Podiatry could never help.

It is not a claim that medication response proves every part of the case.

It is not a claim that every later clinician knew about the alleged Protest.

It is not a claim that an entire institution acted with one shared motive.

It is not a claim that patient memory must be accepted without testing.

The narrower case is stronger.

A major presenting problem remained unresolved. A strong treatment limit was adopted. The patient says he objected before discharge. The completed account did not record that objection. Later neurological evidence repeatedly returned through the feet, but did not visibly reopen the foundation. When the patient finally complained, the institution relied on the continuity produced within that foundation without first proving that the foundation was complete.

## 21. What happened to Liam

The human effect should not be lost inside the documentary argument.

Liam says he raised the unresolved problem while he was still in the hospital. If the Protest occurred, he exercised clinical agency. He asked the team to look again before sending him home.

The future record did not preserve that act.

He then had to live and seek care inside an account that appeared not to contain his objection. Every time the feet returned, he had to explain them again. Medication could change the feet without changing the history. Later persistence could look like a new grievance because the original grievance was absent.

The pain affected walking, daily life and psychological safety. When profound relief finally came, it did not erase those years. It made their possible avoidability newly visible.

The deepest injury alleged is therefore not only delayed relief. It is loss of agency, loss of opportunity, loss of time and loss of control over the official history of the problem.

In plain terms, Liam carried the unresolved question in his body while the record behaved as though the question had been adequately settled.

## 22. The case in one paragraph

Liam entered neurological care because painful feet were affecting his walking. Parkinsonism was identified, but the relationship between the feet and the neurological condition remained unclear without a visible, sustained investigation of that relationship. A senior-backed policy strongly restrained dopaminergic treatment, and Podiatry remained the practical foot route despite years of prior Podiatry. Liam says he protested before discharge that the principal problem had not been dealt with. The completed account did not record that Protest and instead carried forward an apparently coherent and understood plan. Neurological medication later changed the feet repeatedly, and severe OFF-related pain and profound levodopa relief eventually became documented, but the original foundation was not visibly rebuilt. When Liam complained, the hospital relied on the continuity generated within that foundation. The present case asks whether an unresolved and contested choice became an apparently settled history, whether later care and governance depended on that history, and what harm followed.

## 23. The next proper step

The case does not now need a louder accusation.

It needs independent work.

The full records should be preserved and organised. Liam's own proof should be taken carefully. The alleged witnesses should be identified. The Complex Case Meeting material and senior communications should be obtained. The medication timeline should be verified. The complaint and SIMT source schedules should be disclosed. A suitably qualified movement-disorders neurologist should examine the clinical course. A solicitor should protect all possible time limits and decide the proper legal form of the claim.

The central question should remain simple:

> Did the system ever independently return to the unresolved problem that brought Liam into care, or did it keep moving forward inside the answer recorded after he says he protested?

That is what the evidence must now decide.

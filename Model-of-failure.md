# A Model of Systems Failure: From Selective Intake to Recursive Institutional Closure

**1. The system received a clinically unified problem.** The starting point was not an incidental complaint about feet attached retrospectively to a Parkinson’s diagnosis. The GP relationship opened because of bilateral foot pain and impaired walking. The 29 June 2017 referral placed the feet, gait, difficulty initiating movement, bodily stiffness and toe-clawing within the same presenting history. It recorded that Liam attributed his abnormal gait to painful corns and calluses. Neurology therefore received a patient whose principal disabling complaint was located where his body met the ground. Any later account of the admission must begin with that fact.

**2. The admission produced rapid diagnostic movement in one direction but no comparable movement on the feet.** Severe young-onset parkinsonism was recognised early and investigated through neurological examination, imaging and laboratory work. The feet were described structurally—corns, calluses, fallen arches, pes planus, hammer toes—and the gait was recorded as having a foot-drop appearance “somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture.” Yet the disclosed record does not show a corresponding investigation capable of adjudicating whether the painful feet were structural, dystonic, Parkinsonian, medication-responsive or the product of abnormal neurological loading. Listing visible abnormalities recorded the problem; it did not resolve its mechanism.

**3. The disclosed chronology supports the inference that the feet became clinically stationary.** After the initial examination on 3 July, there is no demonstrated sequence of focused reassessment, movement-disorders review, defined OFF examination, therapeutic test or reasoned neurological synthesis directed specifically to the feet. By 6 July, Podiatry had been ordered through the GP record under the operative Parkinson’s diagnosis. The provenance of that order remains important: the presently available material does not establish that Neurology requested it, while the later discharge document itself again asks the GP to arrange Podiatry. The safest conclusion is not that nothing whatsoever occurred, but that no meaningful investigative progress on the principal foot problem is visible in the record supplied.

**4. At the same time, senior authority hardened the treatment architecture.** The 2017 package attributes “the greatest of care” regarding ordinary dopaminergic treatment to consultation with Professor Timothy Counihan and Neurology colleagues. It later states that “Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you’d advise otherwise.” This should not be reduced to the crude assertion that a superior simply ordered that levodopa must never be used. The documented position is nevertheless clear: senior authority stood behind a policy of exceptional restraint, and the course was already framed as one from which another specialist would have to displace the treating team.

**5. Administrative certainty preceded diagnostic confirmation.** On 9 July—two days before physical discharge and four days before the DaTSCAN—the treating neurologist certified the statutory Long-Term Illness documentation under Parkinson’s disease. That may have been clinically defensible shorthand for a highly persuasive Parkinsonian syndrome, but its systems significance is unavoidable. Diagnosis, treatment policy, benefit certification, work advice, referrals and discharge planning were becoming operationally settled before the confirmatory scan and while the relationship between the disabling feet and the neurological syndrome remained expressly unresolved.

**6. The Protest exposed the difference between investigation of the patient and investigation of his problem.** On Liam’s account, he objected before discharge that the hospital had given him the equivalent of a “full NCT” through scans, ultrasounds and blood tests, but was sending him out “on bald tyres”—his feet, where he met the road, remained unaddressed. Properly understood, this was not a demand that medicine produce certainty where exhaustive investigation had honestly failed. It was a contemporaneous allegation that the problem which brought him into care had been recorded at the beginning and then left clinically stationary while the institution completed an extensive investigation of something else.

**7. The Protest created a momentary governance choice.** The available choices were not limited to accepting or rejecting Liam’s preferred diagnosis. The admission could have been extended; the feet could have been re-examined; the dystonic-looking posture could have been escalated to the senior decision-makers; the purpose and limits of Podiatry could have been defined; a cautious diagnostic treatment trial could have been considered; or the unresolved disagreement could simply have been preserved with an assigned responsibility and reopening trigger. Instead, discharge proceeded. The institution selected closure without visibly demonstrating resolution of the question that the Protest had forced back into view.

**8. The authoritative account then removed the evidence that closure had been contested.** The post-Protest discharge consolidation retained “Not clear,” the Podiatry disposition, the prolonged treatment-restraint policy, senior authority, a clear forward plan and assertions of the patient’s good understanding. It did not preserve the material fact that the patient had challenged the sufficiency of that plan before discharge. If the Protest is established as known and deliberately excluded because recording it would have required reconsideration, the omission was not merely incomplete documentation. It was the mechanism by which a contested and still-provisional course was converted into an apparently coherent, accepted and sufficient foundation.

**9. The composite document displays a one-way rule of admission.** The DaTSCAN obtained after physical discharge was incorporated as confirmation “as was expected.” Professor Counihan’s authority and genetics article were incorporated. That article itself connected juvenile PRKN disease with dystonia, symmetry and excellent levodopa responsiveness. Yet the patient’s earlier challenge did not enter, and the contemporaneously observed dystonic-looking lower-limb posture did not visibly reconnect the feet to the patient-specific PRKN synthesis. Confirmation was permitted to travel backwards into the foundational account; contradiction was not.

**10. Later care developed inside the resulting partition.** The subsequent record does not show that clinicians ignored the feet. It shows something more structurally revealing: the feet repeatedly generated neurological evidence and prospective treatment action without that evidence visibly returning to reconstruct the 2017 foundation. Procyclidine benefit, deterioration on withdrawal, restless feet, recurrence of the abnormal foot phenomenon, a movement later described as Parkinsonian, morning “warming up,” possible OFF dystonia, shuffling and pain when medication had not been taken, and the assessment of undertreatment all became clinically actionable. Treatment changed forward. The inherited history did not visibly change backward.

**11. The system thereby distributed the harm while preserving the premise.** Podiatry could address structure and pressure; Neurology could adjust Parkinson’s medication; the GP could rely upon specialist correspondence; and later clinicians could treat current manifestations. Each participant could act locally and sometimes helpfully. Yet no identified actor owned the backward-looking question whether the original foot partition had been inadequately investigated. The unresolved issue became institutionally homeless. Continuity of activity then risked being mistaken for continuity of validation, although much of the later record remained genealogically dependent upon the original synthesis.

**12. By 2023, the consequences had become a patient-safety issue.** The record contains extreme burning pain, a reported “death wish,” a GP warning that Liam was not doing well and a request for review “before we get into trouble here.” In March 2025, Liam described the first 150 steps as “murder” and indicated that he was not willing to live with the condition. This is safely characterised as recurrent death-related or suicidal language over approximately two years, not necessarily continuous suicidality. It nevertheless demonstrates that the disputed foot problem had progressed beyond diagnostic inconvenience into severe physical and psychological danger while remaining under the same inherited architecture.

**13. The March 2025 Sinemet CR response should have constituted a major revalidation event.** The treating neurologist prescribed controlled-release Sinemet specifically around the catastrophic morning foot problem and anticipated further relief. The resulting benefit was described as profound. The neurologist acknowledged that response, said he could only imagine the pain and offered a personal and professional apology. The response did not by itself prove that every component of the foot pain had always been dystonic or that the entire earlier course was negligent. It did, however, supply powerful longitudinal evidence that the feet were neurologically modifiable and required the history to be reconsidered rather than merely the prescription to be altered.

**14. The complaint gave the hospital a second opportunity to break the dependency.** Nine days after the March consultation, Liam complained of diagnostic anchoring, failure to reassess, prolonged preventable suffering and the significance of the Sinemet CR response. At that stage he was not yet alleging an omitted Protest; he had no reason previously to expect every bedside exchange to appear in a discharge summary and had not yet identified the documentary mechanism. The hospital was nevertheless on express notice that the apparent continuity of the case might represent the persistence of an untested premise. It could have returned to the GP referral, raw admission evidence, treatment responses and patient account through an independent clinical reconstruction.

**15. Instead, institutional review completed the recursive closure.** The neurologist’s response explained the course through the continuity of the treatment strategy: responsiveness had been anticipated, restraint had been deliberate, and the later benefit could therefore be accommodated within the original rationale. A Preliminary Assessment Report was presented to SIMT on 27 August 2025, and the outcome communicated on 3 September was “no deficit in care.” The disclosed outcome does not show independent revalidation of the presenting foot problem, the “Not clear” relationship, the Protest, the Podiatry transition, the senior decision provenance or the later state-dependent evidence. In January 2026, QPS continued to rely upon the completed SIMT process and maintained that later dystonia information did not undermine it. The system had therefore travelled from selective intake, through premature operational closure, documentary asymmetry, inherited clinical dependency and local symptom management, to institutional reliance upon its own continuity. The final governance conclusion did not independently prove that the original foundation was sound. On the presently disclosed architecture, it is the final and clearest expression of the systems failure: the history generated by the unresolved decision was used to validate the decision that generated the history.

How the Absence of the Protest Concealed a Decision Point, Converted Clinical Inactivity into Apparent Resolution, and Commissioned the Future Record

Evidence-disciplined case analysis | August 2026

Controlling proposition. If the Protest occurred and was known to the author of the completed discharge account, its omission did more than remove a patient’s disagreement. It removed the event that disclosed that the principal presenting problem had not advanced after the initial assessment, concealed that discharge represented a choice made over an expressly raised alternative, and allowed a contested and clinically stationary foot problem to enter the future record as though it had received a considered disposition.

Purpose and evidential discipline

This document isolates the omission as a separate act and mechanism. It does not attempt to prove every later clinical decision wrong, assign a shared motive to downstream clinicians or convert incompleteness automatically into fraud. It asks what disappeared, what the completed record represented instead, what immediate function the omission performed, and how later records may have inherited the resulting information state.

The analysis proceeds on a necessary distinction:

the patient’s evidence proves the Protest if accepted;

comparison with the completed document proves its absence;

knowledge, materiality and purpose must be established from the circumstances and responsible actors;

the later record proves proposition-specific downstream operation and consequence;

and medical or legal causation requires independent expert and legal analysis.

The omission is therefore neither self-proving nor immaterial merely because it concerns an unrecorded conversation. Its significance depends upon the work the missing event would have performed inside the completed account.

The information state before the Protest

Before discharge, the record contained an unresolved asymmetry.

The Parkinsonism inquiry had moved quickly. Severe akinetic-rigid Parkinsonism was recorded; imaging and laboratory work were performed; the case was considered through a Complex Case Meeting; young-onset and genetic explanations were explored; treatment restraint acquired senior provenance; and Parkinson’s disease acquired operative administrative status.

The feet had a different history. They opened the GP relationship, appeared in the first paragraph of the urgent neurological referral, had been treated in Podiatry since 2009–2010, remained problematic, and were associated with toe-clawing and an abnormal gait. Neurology observed pes planus, hammer toes and a footdrop-like gait suggestive of dystonic posture. It entertained separation or peripheral neuropathy and wrote “Not clear.”

What the accessible record does not demonstrate is a continuing foot-directed investigative sequence between the initial examination and discharge. The secure Podiatry pathway had been initiated by the GP on 10 April and prioritised by the GP on 6 July “as Parkinson’s.” Neurology’s later extended account asked the GP to refer and stated only that the SHO had “tried” in hospital.

The information state was therefore not simply “complex case, uncertain answer.” It may have been “substantially investigated Parkinsonism, initially observed but clinically stationary feet.”

What the Protest would have added to the record

Had it been preserved, the Protest would have added at least seven material facts:

the patient did not regard the dominant presenting problem as investigated;

he distinguished extensive diagnostic activity from attention to the feet;

he regarded the pre-existing Podiatry route as insufficient;

he challenged discharge before it occurred;

a trainee considered the concern sufficiently material to fetch the treating neurologist;

the treating neurologist was placed on notice of the alleged gap;

and discharge proceeded after the alternative of reopening the problem had been expressly raised.

Those facts would have changed the meaning of the completed account. “Not clear” would no longer appear solely as a clinician’s reasoned expression of complexity. It would sit beside the patient’s evidence that the relationship remained unclear because the feet had not been followed after admission.

Podiatry would no longer appear simply as an accepted forward plan. It would appear as the route the patient expressly said did not answer the problem.

“Good understanding” could not be expanded into global agreement. It would have to be read alongside a preserved dissent distinguishing comprehension of Parkinsonism and medication caution from acceptance of the foot disposition and discharge.

What the completed account represented instead

The completed discharge synthesis performed several operations.

It installed Parkinson’s disease as the declarative headline. It provided a lengthy history, detailed examination, extensive differential, treatment architecture, senior provenance and patient counselling. It recorded structural foot findings, possible peripheral explanation and “Not clear.” It included Podiatry in the plan. It stated that the patient had a good understanding of the reason for delaying ordinary dopaminergic treatment. “Plan/Recommendations: As per above” made the narrative itself operational.

The document remained temporally open. The patient was discharged on 11 July. The DaTSCAN was performed on 13 July and entered the composite as expected confirmation. The alleged 10 July Protest, which challenged the sufficiency of discharge, was not entered.

The resulting account therefore preserved:

the diagnosis;

the investigations;

the treatment rationale;

the senior authority;

the Podiatry disposition;

the patient’s understanding;

and later confirmation.

It did not preserve the event showing that the plan was contested because the central problem had not advanced.

The omission concealed a choice

The omission’s deepest significance is not that one conversation was absent. It is that the conversation exposed a fork in the clinical course.

After the Protest, the institution could have delayed discharge, re-examined the feet, sought a defined movement-disorders opinion, tested the dystonic hypothesis, documented why further investigation was unnecessary, established an integrated early review, or preserved the disagreement as an open problem.

It proceeded with discharge.

If the Protest is omitted, that course looks like the natural culmination of the admission. If the Protest is restored, the same course becomes a decision made after the patient expressly identified an unresolved and allegedly uninvestigated problem.

The omission therefore removed the alternative from the history. It preserved the selected branch while deleting the event that showed another branch had been demanded.

The document recorded what was done, but not what had to be declined or left undone in order to do it.

From non-investigation to apparent resolution

The completed account’s breadth creates a particular evidential risk. By recounting the foot history, listing pes planus and hammer toes, mentioning burning and neuropathy, preserving “Not clear” and including Podiatry, it can create the appearance that the feet received longitudinal clinical consideration.

But description distributed across a long document is not necessarily investigation. A catalogue may resemble a pathway when later read as a whole, even if every foot proposition derives from the initial history and examination.

The Protest would have prevented that retrospective compression. It would have told the next clinician:

The feet were recorded, but the patient says they were not followed; the relationship remains unresolved; he does not accept that Podiatry alone answers it; discharge proceeded despite that objection.

Without the Protest, initial observation, unresolved speculation and practical disposition can be read together as a completed clinical process. The omission thus allowed the document to convert clinical inactivity into apparent consideration and disposition into apparent resolution.

This proposition does not require proof that the foot passages were fabricated or written only after the Protest. Version history is required to determine drafting sequence. The functional point is narrower: in its completed form, the account made no distinction between an issue repeatedly investigated and one recorded initially but not visibly pursued.

The narrowing of motive

Motive belongs at the omission, not throughout the subsequent course.

If the Protest occurred, the responsible author knew about it, understood its materiality and nevertheless excluded it, the immediate motive for examination is concrete: recording the Protest would have destabilised the discharge narrative and required an answer to the charge that the central problem had not been investigated.

The possible purpose need not have been to cause future suffering, deceive every later clinician or conceal a known diagnosis. It may have been narrower:

to preserve and complete an immediate senior-backed discharge course without reopening the feet, delaying discharge or recording that the patient disputed the sufficiency of the plan.

The inference would depend upon proof of:

occurrence;

authorial knowledge;

materiality;

completion of the document after the event;

selective inclusion of confirmatory and reassuring material;

exclusion of the challenge;

and the immediate consequence avoided by omission.

An innocent explanation remains possible: oversight, summarisation, belief that the concern had been answered, or a judgment that it was not material. The question is which explanation best fits the document’s purpose, detail, chronology and selectivity. Motive must be inferred, not presumed.

The information state transmitted downstream

The completed account did not need to state expressly that the feet were proven separate. Its structure could transmit a sufficient practical state:

Parkinsonism established and carefully investigated;

usual dopaminergic treatment deliberately restrained;

structural or peripheral possibilities associated with the feet;

Podiatry as the practical foot pathway;

patient understanding recorded;

plan ready to continue.

Later clinicians could rely upon that state in good faith. The omission did not require them to share the originating purpose or even know that a Protest had occurred. They needed only to treat the completed discharge synthesis as the authoritative foundation of the case.

That produces documentary dependency. Repetition of the inherited categories is evidence that they travelled; it is not necessarily independent proof that they were correct at source.

What the later record proves

The subsequent notes do not independently prove the words spoken at the bedside. They show what happened within the information state created after it.

The feet repeatedly returned through Neurology. Procyclidine materially improved the physical state, and withdrawal produced deterioration that included restless feet and a collapsing walk (CLIN-001782–001814). Later records described the foot throw as Parkinsonian, recorded normal power, excessive movement and exquisite foot sensitivity (CLIN-002284–002288). The records later considered toe-curling, possible dystonia and OFF-related symptoms. In 2023, severe bilateral foot and lower-leg suffering was documented as “causing death wish” (CLIN-002637–002641).

By 2025, Parkinson medication was acknowledged to relieve the feet; further Sinemet was expected to provide additional relief; Sinemet CR was prescribed for the severe morning problem; and the response was described as “profound relief” (CLIN-002777–002800; CLIN-003266).

These facts do not automatically prove that a particular 2017 treatment was mandatory. They prove that the foot question remained clinically consequential and repeatedly capable of neurological modification. They also permit examination of whether later evidence produced only prospective adjustment or was ever allowed to return to the disputed 2017 foundation.

Patient consequence

The patient was not merely deprived of an accurate account of one conversation. If the omission installed an apparently uncontested foundation, he was required to seek care inside the history created without his objection.

His original position became harder to prove with time. Later persistence could appear new or repetitive because the first challenge was absent. Cooperation with treatment could be read as acceptance of the framework; resistance could be characterised as difficulty accepting it. The patient carried the unresolved question in his body while the record behaved as though the admission had supplied a sufficient plan.

The suicide-relevant documentation gives the consequence exceptional gravity. The record contains an express 2023 “death wish” associated with the foot and lower-leg suffering and grave 2025 language describing “murder in the feet,” “torture” and “I won’t live with it.” This does not establish continuous suicidal intent over every intervening day or prove legal causation from the 2017 omission. It establishes a two-year span of documentation showing the level of danger and suffering to which the unresolved problem had escalated.

The complaint response and recursive validation

The 25 March 2025 complaint response acknowledged that the pain and discomfort had been documented but stated that the treating clinician had not understood that this was how the patient felt (CLIN-003239). It relied upon the accumulated record to say that Sinemet responsiveness had long been understood, that treatment restraint was deliberate, that Sinemet had been used since 2021 and that the eventual profound response fitted the established strategy (CLIN-003247–003269).

The same response did not visibly reconstruct the Protest, the absence of demonstrated foot-directed investigation, the original dystonic-looking posture or the 2023 death-wish warning as possible impeachment of the inherited foundation.

That creates the central circularity:

The continuity generated within the framework was used to support the framework, while the same continuity was not given equivalent force as evidence that the patient danger and neurological significance of the feet required a return to the beginning.

The later record cannot cure the omission merely by being extensive. To the extent that it inherited the altered decision environment, it is evidence of the omission’s reach rather than independent authentication of the originating account.

The record’s double evidential role

In an ordinary clinical-negligence case, the notes principally evidence what clinicians observed and did. Here, the notes have a double role.

They are evidence of the clinical course. They are also alleged to be part of the mechanism by which a provisional and contested course became decisive. The completed discharge document did not merely report the state of care; it selected the information state that later clinicians would inherit.

Lawyer-to-lawyer formulation

The case is not that the discharge account contains an innocent failure to reproduce every bedside conversation. The alleged omission concerns the one exchange that exposed a material deficit in the work represented by the document. The patient says that, after an extensive Parkinsonism work-up but no demonstrated continuing inquiry into the feet, he objected that the principal functional problem remained untouched and that renewed Podiatry did not answer it. If the author knew of that objection and excluded it, the completed account preserved the chosen discharge course while removing the event that showed the course had been selected over a contemporaneous demand for reconsideration. The later record then inherited an apparently settled information state. Its continuity may therefore prove documentary dependence and consequence rather than independently validate the original disposition.

Formulation for the Court

Judge, the plaintiff’s allegation is not that the medical record should have reproduced every word spoken during an eight-day admission. It is that this omitted exchange was qualitatively different. It challenged the adequacy of discharge, asserted that the central presenting problem had not been followed after the initial examination, rejected Podiatry as a sufficient answer to a problem already treated there for years, and caused the treating neurologist to return to the bedside. If that evidence is accepted, the omission removed a material fact from a document intended to consolidate the admission and govern future care. The completed account then recorded the diagnosis, senior treatment rationale, patient understanding, Podiatry and later scan confirmation, but not the objection that made discharge a contested choice. The Court must determine whether that omission was innocent, whether it materially altered the information conveyed to later clinicians and what difference an accurate account would probably have made.

Evidence required to determine the omission

The decisive sources are:

native EHR creation, access and modification history for the discharge documents;

every draft and printed state of the extended account;

complete ward, nursing and multidisciplinary notes;

trainee allocation and witness evidence;

the Complex Case Meeting record and circulation material;

the full information supplied to Counihan and Healy;

SHO task records and every attempted or completed Podiatry referral;

Podiatry routing, registration and annotation metadata;

evidence explaining the intended scope of “good understanding”;

and independent neurological evidence addressing the likely counterfactual if the Protest had been preserved and acted upon.

Final proposition

The alleged omission did not simply remove the patient’s voice from the history. It removed the event that revealed why the feet were still “Not clear,” concealed that discharge represented a choice to proceed without reopening them, and allowed the final catalogue of history, findings and Podiatry to resemble a completed clinical pathway. If knowing omission is established, the later record cannot authenticate that foundation merely through continuity. It must be examined as the future commissioned by an account that preserved the selected course while deleting the challenge capable of changing it.

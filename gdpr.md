Making 2017 Present: How a Patient Used GDPR to Reopen a Closed Clinical Past

There is a simple way to misunderstand this case. It is to think that the GDPR story began when a patient became dissatisfied with a hospital and started invoking data-protection law.

It did not.

It began with a tablet.

On 10 March 2025, after years of disabling bilateral foot pain, the patient was prescribed Sinemet CR. The response he later described was profound: the foot pain reduced dramatically, walking changed, and the psychological burden associated with the pain eased. The significance was not simply therapeutic. Something that happened to his body in March 2025 changed the way he understood the preceding eight years. The clinical history that had previously appeared to him as a difficult but coherent course suddenly became questionable.

Nine days later, on 19 March, he complained.

That complaint was not yet the mature case he would eventually construct. He did not yet possess the complete records. He did not yet understand the possible importance of the missing July 2017 bedside Protest. He did not yet have a developed theory of documentary dependency or cross-controller processing. He knew something much simpler: a medication had produced a result powerful enough to make him wonder whether the story under which he had been treated had been wrong, incomplete or insufficiently reconsidered.

He notified the hospital and asked it to look backwards.

What happened next gradually turned GDPR from an obscure legal framework into the patient's principal means of making 2017 present again.

The problem with a past that remains active

A medical record is an unusual form of history.

Ordinary history stays in the past. A clinical record does not. A diagnosis entered years earlier may determine which specialist sees a patient today, which medication a GP feels authorised to alter, how a new consultant interprets symptoms, how a complaint is assessed, or what an administrator believes is already settled.

That became increasingly visible during 2025.

The hospital's April response treated the matter as one of clinical judgment and relied substantially on the existing clinical account. But the patient had not recovered simply because he had discovered that Sinemet affected the pain. The contemporaneous material shows continued requests for psychological support, medication-management problems, attempts to obtain treatment for what he believed was dystonia, and difficulty getting primary care to depart from the specialist neurological framework. The later primary-care chronology records a particularly revealing statement from his GP in August: he could not realistically adjust levodopa medication without Neurology guidance.

The patient was therefore discovering two things simultaneously.

He believed his body was telling him something new.

And the institutions around him were still acting from something old.

That is the point at which data protection became practically important.

Article 16: trying to create a before and an after

The first major GDPR move came on 12 June 2025.

The patient invoked Article 16 and asked for rectification and annotation of allegedly inaccurate or incomplete health data held by Galway University Hospitals. He challenged the way the bilateral foot problem had been represented, the peripheral explanations attached to it, and the apparent conclusiveness of the inherited diagnostic history.

Legally, Article 16 concerns rectification of inaccurate personal data and completion of incomplete data.

But in the lived story of this case, its function was more interesting.

The patient was trying to force the record to acquire a temporal break.

He needed it to become possible to say:

This is what the institution believed before March 2025.

And:

This is what happened in March 2025 that materially challenged that understanding.

Without that distinction, the new treatment response could simply be absorbed into the existing chronology. The medication could change while the historical explanation remained intact. The patient could improve while the record continued to imply continuity.

Article 16 became his attempt to stop that happening.

The object was not necessarily to erase the historical fact that a clinician had once held a particular opinion. It was to prevent the historical opinion from continuing to function as unqualified present truth.

That distinction would eventually become one of the central disputes in the case.

Then the patient discovered that the record itself had a history

Three days later, on 15 June, the patient made another Article 16 request. This one was directed not merely at clinical information but at the Quality & Patient Safety record: the language used to describe complaint assessment and closure.

That step is easy to overlook, but it marks an important development.

The patient had begun to understand that institutions do not create only one record.

There was the clinical record.

Then there was a complaints record explaining what the institution had supposedly done when the clinical record was challenged.

If the second record described the first as having been properly assessed and resolved, it too could become authoritative. The institution could accumulate layers of apparent confirmation without ever returning to the originating question.

So the patient's GDPR activity began spreading outward.

He was no longer attempting merely to correct a diagnosis.

He was attempting to prevent institutional descriptions of the dispute from becoming substitutes for examination of the dispute itself.

Article 15: leave the document and find the people

The most revealing move came on 7 July 2025.

The patient submitted an Article 15 access request concerning a bedside teaching interaction in July 2017. He sought not only clinical documentation but student logbooks, teaching records, supervisory material, ward-round material and records capable of identifying the trainees present.

This was different from everything before it.

By then, the patient had begun to appreciate the possible significance of an event he says occurred shortly before discharge in 2017.

His account is that four trainees were present when he objected that the hospital had extensively investigated him but had failed to address the disabling problem with his feet; one trainee allegedly went to fetch the treating neurologist. The available discharge account does not preserve that Protest.

If the patient's evidence is accepted, the importance of the omission is obvious. The missing event would show that the unresolved feet were not merely something discovered retrospectively in 2025. The patient had allegedly challenged their treatment before discharge in 2017.

But there was now another realization.

The Protest might be absent from the chart, but it had allegedly occurred in front of four other human beings.

That changed the evidential landscape.

Article 15 gave the patient a lawful way to ask whether the real-world event had left traces outside the authoritative clinical narrative.

Who were the students?

Was there a teaching roster?

Did anyone write a logbook entry?

Was the case discussed?

Did a supervisor record anything?

Was there an educational trail independent of the discharge account?

The GDPR exercise had ceased to be primarily about correcting words.

It had become a method of reconstructing reality around an omission.

Article 18: stopping the past from reproducing itself

By August, the case had moved into a Serious Incident Management Team process.

On 4 August, while the accuracy dispute remained unresolved, the patient invoked Article 18 restriction. He asked that disputed diagnostic information be restricted and sought clarity about the data sources, versions and audit trail being used in the institutional review.

Conceptually, this was the next logical step.

Article 16 had said:

This information is disputed. Correct or qualify it.

Article 18 now said:

While that dispute is unresolved, do not allow the disputed information to keep producing new consequences.

That mattered because the hospital was no longer merely storing the old record. It was processing it in order to produce something new: a governance determination.

The danger, from the patient's perspective, was recursive.

An allegedly incomplete 2017 account could generate eight years of downstream clinical history.

That downstream history could then be placed before SIMT.

SIMT could then produce a fresh institutional conclusion that there had been no deficit in care.

And that new conclusion could thereafter be cited as additional evidence that the original history was sound.

The record would effectively validate itself through its descendants.

Article 18 was the patient's attempt to interrupt that reproduction.

Sometimes accuracy means preserving the error

One of the most revealing GDPR interventions occurred in September 2025.

The patient became aware of a statement that he had allegedly not wanted contact with clinicians. He regarded the statement as demonstrably inaccurate.

But he did something unexpected.

He did not ask for it to be deleted.

Instead, on 29 September, he invoked Article 18 and asked that the statement be preserved, identified and flagged as disputed. He expressly explained that he wanted the line to remain visible because he regarded it as evidentially important.

That episode captures how sophisticated the patient's use of GDPR had become.

Early in the process, accuracy meant correction.

Now he understood that an inaccurate record could itself be evidence.

Removing the sentence might make the current file cleaner while destroying information about how the institutional account had evolved.

So the desired remedy changed:

not erase the error, but preserve the error without allowing it to govern.

That is the distinction between documentary correction and evidential preservation.

The University: taking the Protest outside the hospital

By November 2025, the search for the four trainees had developed into a second-controller strategy.

The hospital's Article 15 response had not produced the hoped-for teaching records. The patient then turned to the University of Galway.

On 24 November he sent a combined Article 15, 16 and 18 request concerning the July 2017 teaching interaction.

Under Article 15, he sought trainee records, logbooks, case-based discussions, supervisory material and academic governance documentation.

Under Article 16, he asked that inaccurate or incomplete teaching representations be corrected, including any suggestion that his foot concern had been addressed or resolved when, on his account, it had not.

Under Article 18, he sought restriction on any further teaching, presentation, analysis or academic use of the case while those accuracy issues remained unresolved.

The request expressly described four trainee doctors participating in the teaching interaction and sought University-held material arising from it.

This was the most literal attempt yet to make 2017 present.

The patient's proposition was no longer:

Believe my memory instead of the hospital record.

It was:

There were other people there. There may have been another institution involved. Search its information systems.

That is a fundamentally different evidential move.

The missing Protest had started as a contest between memory and document.

GDPR allowed the patient to convert it into a search across institutional boundaries.

Podiatry: following another branch of 2017

The same method eventually reached Podiatry.

The feet had been central to the 2017 presentation, and Podiatry had later become part of the hospital's account of what was done with them.

In July 2026, after earlier correspondence had clarified that relevant Podiatry material was held through another HSE service, the patient submitted a fresh Article 15 request to obtain the Podiatry/Chiropody record.

He asked for referrals, referral-processing material, clinical records, waiting-list and appointment records, closure or discharge records, communications with Neurology and the GP, and information about sources and recipients of the data.

Again the underlying question was historical but the mechanism was present-day.

What exactly was Podiatry asked to do in 2017?

Who initiated the pathway?

What information was transmitted?

Was the foot problem being investigated neurologically, structurally, or simply passed onward?

What came back?

The patient's Article 15 request sought to reconstruct a part of 2017 not from the later hospital synthesis but from the separate service that had actually handled the feet.

When he considered the eventual response incomplete, he escalated that issue to the DPC under Article 77 in August 2026.

Fighting 2017 with the law of the present

There is a broader idea here.

By 2025, the patient could not physically return to July 2017.

He could not recreate the ward.

He could not compel memory.

He could not undo the discharge.

And if records had never been created—or had no longer survived—GDPR could not magically manufacture them.

But he discovered that 2017 was not truly gone.

It was still being processed.

The old diagnosis still influenced treatment.

The old discharge account still informed later understanding.

The old history could be used in complaint assessment.

It could be used by SIMT.

It could appear in primary care.

It might have travelled into teaching.

It had travelled into administrative systems.

And that gave the patient an opportunity.

If an old representation continues to be processed now, questions about its present accuracy, completeness, provenance, recipients and use are not purely historical questions.

That is how GDPR became, for this patient, a means of fighting 2017 in 2025 and 2026.

Article 15 allowed him to ask where 2017 had gone.

Article 16 allowed him to ask whether the surviving version of 2017 remained accurate enough to govern the present.

Article 18 allowed him to say stop the disputed version from generating further consequences while we determine that.

Article 19 later offered a way to ask where corrections or restrictions would have to travel if the old account had already propagated.

And Article 77 allowed him to take the dispute outside the institutions that had generated the underlying records.

The strategy was not to abolish history.

It was to make history contestable while it was still alive.

Making the missing event substantially present

That is ultimately what links the tablet, the complaint, the Protest, the University, Podiatry and the regulator.

The patient could never place the DPC investigator physically in St Anthony's Ward in July 2017.

What he could do was increasingly surround the missing moment with surviving evidence.

He could put before the regulator the GP referral that began with the feet.

He could put forward the neurological record describing the abnormal gait and unresolved relationship.

He could show the discharge account that did not contain the Protest.

He could identify the alleged four witnesses.

He could ask the hospital for their traces.

He could ask the University for independent teaching traces.

He could follow the Podiatry pathway into another controller's records.

He could place the March 2025 pharmacological event beside the old history.

He could preserve the later institutional responses.

He could challenge the accuracy of the information being used.

And he could ask that disputed data stop generating still more authoritative conclusions.

No single one of those actions proves what happened in 2017.

Together, however, they do something more interesting.

They increase the documentary density around the missing event.

They make it progressively harder for the only surviving question to be:

What does the 2017 discharge summary say?

The question becomes:

What was the actual information state in July 2017, who encountered it, what traces did it produce, what disappeared from the authoritative account, where did that account travel, and what happened when the patient finally challenged it?

That is what the patient's GDPR campaign accomplished at its most fundamental level.

He could not reopen the ward.

So he reopened the information environment around the ward.

He could not bring 2025 physically back to 2017.

Instead, through access, rectification, restriction, preservation and regulatory complaint, he brought as much of 2017 forward into 2025 and 2026 as the surviving record would permit.

And once he had done that, the old clinical history was no longer merely a closed account of what happened.

It had become the subject of a live contest over what the present was entitled to believe about the past.

The case is not fundamentally that the Parkinsonism diagnosis was wrong, that Podiatry was inherently inappropriate, or that every subsequent clinician acted negligently. It concerns the consequences of an allegedly material omission at a decisive point in the clinical record.

In July 2017, the neurological disorder had been identified, but the relationship between it and the patient’s dominant disabling foot problem remained expressly unresolved. The record described a dystonic-looking lower-limb posture, raised PRKN/PARK2 and contained specialist material connecting that phenotype with dystonia and levodopa responsiveness. Nevertheless, the feet passed operationally to Podiatry and ordinary dopaminergic treatment was to be deferred.

The patient’s evidence is that, before discharge, he expressly challenged the adequacy of that disposition. He did not deny Parkinsonism. He said, in substance, that the principal disabling problem remained unexplained and that the proposed foot pathway did not resolve it. That evidence presently requires corroboration, but if accepted it was plainly material to discharge, the clinical status of the feet and whether the patient agreed with the proposed plan.

The critical factual issue is what happened to that Protest. The authoritative discharge account was completed after the event and presented the plan as coherent, senior-supported and understood by the patient. It incorporated later confirmatory information, including the post-discharge DaTSCAN, but did not record the earlier challenge. The plaintiff’s case would be that this was not an immaterial omission: it removed the fact that the clinical disposition was contested at the precise point at which further consideration could still have occurred.

Knowledge and purpose would have to be proved. If the author knew of the Protest and omitted it because recording it would have required reconsideration or impeded discharge, the resulting record was not merely incomplete. It conveyed a materially misleading state of affairs: that an unresolved and disputed clinical transition had been sufficiently explained and accepted.

The downstream case does not require proof that later clinicians knew of or participated in the original omission. It is a reliance case. Later clinicians were entitled to receive an accurate account of the admission. To the extent that they relied upon the completed discharge synthesis, their later repetition of its assumptions cannot constitute independent corroboration of those assumptions. It may demonstrate documentary dependency instead.

The subsequent records are relevant because the disputed foot phenomenon repeatedly returned with neurological features and medication responsiveness. Procyclidine affected the feet, tension and gait. Later records considered Parkinsonian movement, toe-curling, dystonia and OFF-related symptoms. Sinemet relieved the feet; Sinemet CR was ultimately prescribed for the morning foot problem and produced what the treating clinician described as profound relief. The contention is not that this retrospectively proves what the correct 2017 diagnosis or treatment must have been. It is that this evidence was capable of requiring reconsideration of the original partition, yet no such foundational reconstruction is visible.

Causation must be approached carefully. The plaintiff would require expert evidence addressing what a competent clinician should have done had the Protest been recorded, whether that would probably have led to further examination, senior review, a defined reassessment mechanism or an earlier diagnostic or therapeutic trial, and whether any such course would probably have avoided or reduced the subsequent suffering. The omission cannot simply be assumed to have caused every later event.

The damage case is nevertheless potentially grave. In 2023, an urgent referral expressly recorded that the bilateral foot and lower-leg suffering was “causing death wish” and warned of the need for review “before we get into trouble here.” In 2025, the foot experience was recorded as “murder,” “torture” and something the patient “won’t live with.” That establishes suicide-relevant documentation over a two-year span, although it does not without further evidence establish continuous suicidal intent or the legal cause of that mental state.

The complaint response is important principally as evidence of the record’s downstream operation. It relied upon the accumulated clinical history to say that Sinemet responsiveness had long been understood, that restraint had been deliberate and that the eventual response was consistent with the prior approach. But it did not visibly reconstruct the disputed Protest, the original unresolved foot relationship or the documented death-wish warning. The plaintiff would say that the response treated the continuity produced within the inherited framework as validation of the framework itself.

The legal case must therefore be proved link by link: the Protest occurred; it was known to the responsible author; it was material; its omission rendered the completed account materially incomplete or misleading; identifiable later decisions relied upon propositions derived from that account; competent reconsideration would probably have altered the course; and the difference between the actual and counterfactual courses caused identifiable injury.

The case should not presently be pleaded as an eight-year conspiracy. Its strength lies in its narrower architecture: an originating omission, an altered documentary foundation, downstream reliance without necessarily shared fault, repeated missed opportunities for reconsideration, serious patient injury and a complaint process that may have mistaken inherited consistency for independent validation.
